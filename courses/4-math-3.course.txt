## Trigonometry as ratios   @m3.trig
   can: Read the six trigonometric functions as ratios of two lengths, relate them to one another, and use them to measure shapes.

### Ratio objects   @m3.trig.ratio
    can: Name each trigonometric ratio by the two lengths it compares.

- A ratio belongs to the angle   @m3.trig.ratio.same
  can: Explain why a ratio of sides depends only on the angle.
  bite: Scale a right triangle up or down and every side grows by the same factor, so the ratio of any two sides does not change. The ratio depends on the angle and on nothing else. That is why a ratio object can be dragged larger or smaller, or carried anywhere in a layout, and still show the same number.

    ( ) it becomes a third
    ( ) it grows by nine
  ? choice: Two right triangles share an acute angle of 35°. What must be true?
    (x) the ratios between corresponding sides are equal
    ( ) the triangles are the same size
    ( ) their hypotenuses are equal
    ( ) their areas are equal

- Three shapes, six functions   @m3.trig.ratio.shapes
  can: Match each ratio-object shape and polarity to its function.
  bite: A right triangle has three sides, so there are three ways to pick two of them, and a ratio object draws just the two it compares. The fan draws the hypotenuse and the adjacent leg, both leaving the angle: cosine. The elbow draws the adjacent leg and then the opposite leg, meeting at the right angle: tangent. The ramp draws the hypotenuse and the opposite leg: sine. Clicking the object flips its polarity, which swaps which length is measured against which, and gives the reciprocal: secant, cotangent, cosecant. A fourth shape, the scythe, draws an arc and its radius. Its ratio, arc over radius, is the angle itself in radians.
  ? match: Match each pair of lengths to the function that compares them.

    | opposite over adjacent        = tangent
    | opposite over hypotenuse      = sine
    | hypotenuse over adjacent      = secant
    | adjacent over opposite        = cotangent
    | hypotenuse over opposite      = cosecant
  ? match: Match each shape to the two lengths it draws.
    | fan        = hypotenuse and adjacent leg
    | elbow      = adjacent leg and opposite leg
    | ramp       = hypotenuse and opposite leg
    | scythe     = arc and radius

    | sine        = cosecant

### How the six relate   @m3.trig.pairs
    standard: F-TF.C.8
    can: Move from any one of the six functions to any other.

- Three kinds of pair   @m3.trig.pairs.two
  can: Name each function's reciprocal, Pythagorean and complementary partner.
  bite: Put the six functions at the corners of a hexagon. Reciprocal pairs sit opposite each other, and each pair lives on one shape: sine and cosecant on the ramp, tangent and cotangent on the elbow, cosine and secant on the fan. Pythagorean pairs are neighbors that join two different shapes, and there is one for each pair of shapes: sine with cosine, tangent with secant, cotangent with cosecant. They are tied by $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$, $\tan^2 + 1 = \sec^2$ and $\cot^2 + 1 = \csc^2$. Complementary pairs are the ones named with "co-": sine and cosine, tangent and cotangent, secant and cosecant. Use the buttons to light each kind.

    | secant      = cosine
  ? row: Name the Pythagorean partner.

    | tangent       = secant
    | cotangent     = cosecant
  ? row: Name the complementary partner.
    | sine        = cosine
    | tangent     = cotangent
    | secant      = cosecant

    | cotangent and cosecant     = $\cot^2 + 1 = \csc^2$

- From any function to any other   @m3.trig.pairs.path
  can: Find the Pythagorean pair behind any two functions and write one in terms of the other.
  bite: Any two of the six functions can be written in terms of each other. Either they are the same function, or they are direct reciprocals (same shape), or they live on two different shapes. In that last case exactly one Pythagorean pair joins those two shapes, and your two functions are that pair after zero, one or two reciprocal flips. Tangent and secant are a Pythagorean pair as they stand: zero flips. Tangent and cosine: flip cosine to secant, one flip. Cotangent and cosine: flip both, two flips. So to write cosine from cotangent: flip to tangent, use $\sec^2 = \tan^2 + 1$, flip secant to cosine. (A square root gives the size; the quadrant gives the sign.)
  ? row: Which Pythagorean pair is behind each pair of functions?
    | cosecant, cotangent     = cotangent and cosecant
    | tangent, cosecant       = cotangent and cosecant
    | sine, cotangent         = cotangent and cosecant
    | cosecant, secant        = sine and cosine
    | secant, sine            = sine and cosine
    | secant, tangent         = tangent and secant
    | sine, tangent           = cotangent and cosecant
    | cotangent, cosine       = tangent and secant
  ? row: How many reciprocal flips does it take to reach that Pythagorean pair?
    slider: 0 | 1 | 2
    | tangent, secant         = 0
    | tangent, cosine         = 1
    | cotangent, cosine       = 2
    | cosecant, secant        = 2
    | sine, cotangent         = 1
    | sine, cosine            = 0
  ? row: What kind of pair is each?
    | sine, cosecant          = direct reciprocals
    | cosine, cosine          = the same function
    | secant, tangent         = Pythagorean pair
    | tangent, cotangent      = direct reciprocals
  ? choice: Which gives cotangent from cosecant (as a size)?

  ? choice: Which gives cosecant from tangent (as a size)?
    (x) $\sqrt{1 + 1/\tan^2}$

    ( ) $\sqrt{1 - 1/\tan^2}$

### What the functions forget   @m3.trig.inv

- Inverse-obverse composition   @m3.trig.inv.comp
  can: Find what $\cos^{-1}(\cos\theta)$ and $\sin^{-1}(\sin\theta)$ return.
  figure: wrap
  bite: Sine and cosine take an angle and return information about it, but some is lost: whole laps make no difference, for one. The figure winds the whole number line of the angle around a center so that you can see what each composition keeps. So an inverse function cannot return the original angle. It returns a default angle that has the same value. For the cosine pair, the default keeps how far the angle is from the zero direction: $\cos^{-1}(\cos\theta)$ is the angle of the minor arc, between 0° and 180°, with laps and the sense of turning forgotten. For the sine pair, the default lies between −90° and 90°: it keeps how far the angle is above or below the horizontal.

### Measuring with ratios   @m3.trig.use
    can: Use a ratio to find a length or an area.

- Regular polygons   @m3.trig.use.poly
  can: Find the area of a regular polygon from its edge length.
  bite: Cut a regular polygon with $n$ sides into $n$ equal triangles meeting at the center. Each has the edge $L$ as its base and a central angle of $360°/n$. Halve one and you have a right triangle with the angle $180°/n$ at the center and $L/2$ across from it. Its other leg, the height of the triangle, is $\tfrac{L}{2}\cot(180°/n)$. That center-to-edge-midpoint length is the apothem. The hypotenuse, from the center to a vertex, is the circumradius: $\tfrac{L}{2}\csc(180°/n)$. So each triangle has area $\tfrac{L^2}{4}\cot(180°/n)$, and the polygon has $n$ of them.
  ? row: For a regular pentagon cut this way, how many degrees is:
    | the central angle of one triangle        = 72
    | the angle at the center after halving    = 36
  ? match: Match each line in a regular polygon to its name.
    | center to the midpoint of an edge     = apothem
    | center to a vertex                    = circumradius
    | vertex to the next vertex             = edge
  ? row: With the half-angle $180°/n$ at the center and $L/2$ across from it, which function of the half-angle gives each length when multiplied by $L/2$?
    | apothem          = cotangent
    | circumradius     = cosecant
  ? numeric: A regular hexagon has edge length [L = 2 | 4 | 6 | 10]. How long is its apothem?
    = L * sqrt(3) / 2
    why: Half the edge times $\cot 30° = \sqrt3$.
  ? numeric: A regular pentagon has edge length $L$. Its area is what number times $L^2$?
    = 1.7205
    why: $\tfrac54\cot 36°$.
  ? numeric: A regular dodecagon has edge length $L$. Its area is what number times $L^2$?
    = 11.196
    why: $\tfrac{12}{4}\cot 15° = 3 \times 3.732$.
  ? numeric: A regular heptagon has edge length $L$. Its area is what number times $L^2$?
    = 3.634
    why: $\tfrac74\cot(180°/7)$.
  ? numeric: What is the ratio of the area of a regular octagon of edge $L$ to the area of a square of edge $L$?
    = 4.828
    why: $2\cot 22.5° = 2 + 2\sqrt2$.

### Compound angles   @m3.trig.sum
    can: Expand the cosine or sine of a sum of angles by picking cosine or sine from each.

- Every term picks one from each angle   @m3.trig.sum.pick
  can: Count and sign the terms in the expansion of a sum of angles.
  bite: Stack ratio objects, each one built on an arm of the one before. At each angle you either stay along the arm, which costs a factor of cosine, or step off sideways, which costs a factor of sine and turns you a quarter turn. After $n$ angles there are $2^n$ ways through, each a product of $n$ factors. A product with no sines points along the start. One sine: a quarter turn, the sine direction. Two sines: a half turn, so it counts against the cosine. Three: against the sine. That gives $\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta$ and $\sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$. The count of products with $k$ sines is a binomial coefficient.
  links: @m3.binom.count.choose
  ? numeric: Expanding for three angles, how many products are there in all?
    = 8
  ? row: With three angles, how many products have this many sine factors?
    | none      = 1
    | one       = 3
    | two       = 3
    | three     = 1
  ? row: Where does a product with this many sine factors go?
    | none      = cosine, added
    | one       = sine, added
    | two       = cosine, subtracted
    | three     = sine, subtracted
  ? choice: Which is $\cos(\alpha + \beta)$?
    (x) $\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta$
    ( ) $\cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta$
    ( ) $\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$
    ( ) $\cos\alpha + \cos\beta$

## The central dogma of algebra   @m3.dogma
   can: Build addition, multiplication and exponentiation by repetition and predict how they interact.

### Three operations, each from the last   @m3.dogma.build
    standard: N-RN.A.1
    can: Say how each operation is repeated use of the one before.

- Iteration   @m3.dogma.build.iter
  can: Describe each operation as repeating the one below it.
  bite: Start with incrementation: adding one. Repeat it and you have addition, since $a + 3$ is "add one, three times". Repeat addition and you have multiplication: $a \cdot 3$ is $a + a + a$. Repeat multiplication and you have exponentiation: $a^3$ is $a \cdot a \cdot a$. Addition, multiplication and exponentiation are the three operations whose interactions make up most of algebra.
  ? match: Match each operation to what it repeats.
    | addition           = incrementation
    | multiplication     = addition
    | exponentiation     = multiplication
  ? row: Write as a single operation.
    | $a + a + a + a$                    = $4a$
    | $a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a$        = $a^4$
  ? choice: $5 \cdot 3$ unpacks one level down as:
    (x) $5 + 5 + 5$
    ( ) $5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5$
    ( ) $5 + 3$
    ( ) $3^5$

### How the operations interact   @m3.dogma.rules
    standard: A-SSE.A.2 A-SSE.B.3c N-RN.A.2
    can: Predict what happens when one operation meets another.

- Regrouping and reordering   @m3.dogma.rules.props
  can: Say what the associative and commutative properties allow, and which operations have them.
  bite: Draw an expression as a tree: an operation on top, the things it combines underneath. Two changes to the tree's shape are worth naming. Regrouping moves a grouping from one pair to another. Reordering swaps two things under the same operation. Addition and multiplication allow both without changing the value, so their trees can be drawn flat, with no inner grouping at all. Try both changes under each operation, and then try them on a power.
  figure: structure properties
  ? row: Does the value stay the same?
    | regrouping a sum              = yes
    | reordering a product          = yes
    | regrouping a power            = no
    | reordering a power            = no
  ? match: Match each property to the change it allows.
    | associative      = moving the grouping
    | commutative      = swapping the order
  ? numeric: Raise [a = 2 | 3 | 5] to the power [b = 3 | 2]. Then raise the result to the power [c = 2 | 3]. What do you get?
    = a^(b * c)
    why: A power of a power multiplies the exponents: [a] to the power [= b * c].
  ? numeric: What is $2^{(3^2)}$?
    = 512
  ? choice: Why can $A + B + C$ be written with no parentheses at all?
    (x) addition is associative, so every grouping gives the same value
    ( ) addition is always done left to right
    ( ) parentheses are optional in algebra
    ( ) it cannot; a grouping is always implied

- Multiplication over addition   @m3.dogma.rules.dist
  can: Distribute a factor over a sum and say why it works.
  links: @m3.dogma.rules.props
  bite: A count on a sum is the sum repeated. Take $3(A + B)$ apart in the figure: unpack the count, remove the inner groupings, swap terms until like ones are together, group them, and pack each group back into a count. Every step is one of the two properties, and the result is $3A + 3B$. Distribution is what a repeated sum looks like after regrouping and reordering.
  figure: structure distribute
  ? typed: Expand $4(x + y)$.
    = 4x + 4y
    = 4y + 4x
  ? choice: Why does $3(a + b) = 3a + 3b$?
    (x) three copies of the sum, regrouped, hold three $a$'s and three $b$'s
    ( ) multiplication always goes first
    ( ) it is a definition with no reason behind it
    ( ) because $a$ and $b$ are like terms

- Exponentiation over multiplication   @m3.dogma.rules.pow
  can: Distribute an exponent over a product.
  bite: The same reasoning one level up. $(ab)^3$ means $ab \cdot ab \cdot ab$. Regroup and there are three $a$'s and three $b$'s multiplied: $a^3 b^3$. An exponent distributes over a product for the same reason a factor distributes over a sum.
  ? typed: Expand $(xy)^4$.
    = x^4 y^4
    = x^4y^4
  ? numeric: What is the cube of the product [a = 2 | 3 | 4] × [b = 5 | 10]?
    = (a * b)^3
    why: Cube each factor and multiply, or cube the product [= a * b] directly.

- Adding and multiplying exponents   @m3.dogma.rules.exp
  can: Combine exponents on the same base.
  bite: $a^2 \cdot a^3$ is two $a$'s times three $a$'s: five in all, so $a^5$. A sum in the exponent is a product below. $(a^2)^3$ is three copies of two $a$'s: six in all, so $a^6$. A product in the exponent is a power of a power.
  ? row: Simplify to a single power of $a$: give the exponent.
    | $a^2 \cdot a^5$       = 7
    | $(a^2)^5$             = 10
    | $a^3 \cdot a^3$       = 6
    | $(a^3)^3$             = 9

    | $a^{m+n}$      = $a^m \cdot a^n$

    | $(ab)^n$       = $a^n b^n$

- The one that does not distribute   @m3.dogma.rules.not
  can: Recognize that an exponent does not distribute over a sum.
  bite: Distribution works between neighbors: multiplication over addition, exponentiation over multiplication. It does not skip a level. $(a + b)^2$ is not $a^2 + b^2$. Try numbers: $(1 + 2)^2 = 9$, but $1^2 + 2^2 = 5$. What $(a + b)^n$ actually becomes is the subject of the next unit.
  ? choice: Which of these is false in general?
    (x) $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2$
    ( ) $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$
    ( ) $2(a + b) = 2a + 2b$
    ( ) $a^2 \cdot a^3 = a^5$
  ? numeric: What is $(3 + 4)^2 - (3^2 + 4^2)$?
    = 24
    why: 49 − 25. The missing 24 is $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4$, the middle term.
  ? row: Does the outer operation distribute over the inner one?
    | a factor over a sum               = yes
    | an exponent over a product        = yes
    | an exponent over a sum            = no

- True or false   @m3.dogma.rules.tf
  can: Judge a claimed rewriting of a power, product or sum.
  bite: Two questions settle each case. Which operation is outside and which is inside? Are they neighbors, with the outside one built from the inside one? A factor outside a sum distributes. An exponent outside a product distributes. An exponent outside a sum does not, and neither does a factor outside a product: $3(ABC)$ is three copies of $ABC$ added, not $3A \cdot 3B \cdot 3C$. In the exponent, a sum becomes a product of powers and a product becomes a power of a power.

    | $A^{2+3+7} = A^2 \cdot A^3 \cdot A^7$              = true
    | $A^{2+3+7} = A^2 + A^3 + A^7$                      = false
    | $3(ABC) = 3A \cdot 3B \cdot 3C$                    = false
    | $(ABC)^4 = A^4 \cdot B^4 \cdot C^4$                = true
    | $13(A+B+C) = 13A + 13B + 13C$                      = true
  ? row: True or false?
    | $A^{3 \cdot 5 \cdot 2} = A^3 \cdot A^5 \cdot A^2$  = false
    | $A^{3 \cdot 5 \cdot 2} = ((A^3)^5)^2$              = true
    | $A^{3 \cdot 5 \cdot 2} = ((A^5)^2)^3$              = true
  ? row: Can the outside operation be distributed directly over the inside?
    | $(x+2)^7$          = no

## Counting   @m3.count
   can: Count arrangements by multiplying choices and by dividing out what cannot be told apart.

### Slots and mappings   @m3.count.slot
    can: Count the ways to fill slots and the mappings between two sets.

- Independent and dependent choices   @m3.count.slot.fill
  can: Count the ways to fill slots with and without repetition.
  bite: Fill slots one at a time and multiply the number of choices at each. If items can be repeated, every slot has the same choices and the choices are independent: 5 items in 4 slots gives $5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5 = 5^4$. If an item is used up when chosen, each slot has one choice fewer than the last and the choices depend on each other: 7 items in 5 slots gives $7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3$, a factorial cut short.
  ? numeric: Five items fill four slots, and items can be repeated. How many ways?
    = 625
  ? numeric: [n = 7 | 6 | 8] items fill [k = 5 | 3 | 4] slots, and an item is used up once chosen. How many ways?
    = fact(n) / fact(n - k)
    why: Start at [n] and count down, one factor per slot: $n!/(n-k)!$.
  ? row: Are the choices independent or dependent?
    | items can be repeated                    = independent
    | items are used up once chosen            = dependent
  ? numeric: [n = 6 | 4 | 5 | 8] items fill 3 slots, and items can be repeated. How many ways?
    = n^3

- Mappings of a bipartition   @m3.count.slot.map
  can: Count the mappings between two sets from the degrees of their members.
  bite: Put the things being arranged in one row, each with degree 1: it takes part once. Put what they are assigned to in a second row, each with a degree saying how many it takes. With 5 things above and five distinct labels below, each of degree 1, there are $5!$ mappings. If a label below has degree 3, the three things it takes can be swapped among themselves without changing anything, so divide by $3!$. In general, the count is the factorial of the total divided by the factorial of every degree: $\tfrac{N!}{A!\,B!}$ when the degrees are $A$ and $B$ with $A + B = N$.
  ? row: How many mappings? Above: distinct things of degree 1. Below: labels with the degrees given.
    | 5 above; below, five labels of degree 1               = 120
    | 5 above; below, Y of degree 3 and N of degree 2       = 10
    | 5 above; below, three labels of degree 1 and N of degree 2    = 60
    | 7 above; below, degrees 3, 2 and 2                    = 210
  ? numeric: There are [n = 5 | 6 | 7] distinguishable light switches on a panel. In how many ways can exactly [k = 3 | 2] be on and the rest off?
    = fact(n) / (fact(k) * fact(n - k))
    why: $\tfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ with $n$ = [n] and $k$ = [k].
  ? choice: Why divide by $3!$ when a label has degree 3?
    (x) the three things it takes can be reordered among themselves without changing the mapping
    ( ) three of the things are left out
    ( ) each thing can be used three times
    ( ) there are three labels

## Expanding binomials   @m3.binom
   standard: A-APR.C.4 A-SSE.A.2
   can: Expand a power of a binomial by counting.

### Expansion is counting   @m3.binom.count
    can: Find a coefficient by counting choices.

- One choice from each factor   @m3.binom.count.choose
  can: Explain where the terms of an expansion come from.
  links: @m3.dogma.rules.not
  bite: $(a + b)^3$ is $(a + b)(a + b)(a + b)$. Each term of the expansion is made by picking $a$ or $b$ from each of the three factors and multiplying the picks. The term $a^2 b$ shows up once for every way to pick $b$ from exactly one factor: three ways. So its coefficient is 3. A coefficient is a count of ways to choose.
  ? numeric: In $(a + b)^3$, how many ways are there to pick $b$ from exactly one of the three factors?
    = 3
  ? numeric: How many terms are there in $(a + b)^4$ before like terms are collected?
    = 16
    why: Two choices from each of four factors: $2^4$.
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $a^2 b^2$ in $(a + b)^4$?
    = 6
    why: Choose which two of the four factors give $b$: 6 ways.

- n choose k   @m3.binom.count.formula
  can: Compute a binomial coefficient.
  links: @m3.count.slot.map
  bite: The number of ways to choose $k$ things out of $n$ is written $\binom{n}{k}$ and equals $\tfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$. In practice, write $k$ falling factors starting at $n$ on top and $k!$ below: $\binom{5}{2} = \tfrac{5 \cdot 4}{2 \cdot 1} = 10$. Choosing which $k$ to take is the same as choosing which $n - k$ to leave, so $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$.
  ? row: Evaluate.
    | $\binom{5}{2}$      = 10
    | $\binom{6}{3}$      = 20
    | $\binom{7}{1}$      = 7
    | $\binom{7}{6}$      = 7
    | $\binom{4}{0}$      = 1
  ? numeric: What is $\binom{n}{k}$ when $n$ = [n = 10 | 9 | 12] and $k$ = [k = 8 | 7]?
    = fact(n) / (fact(k) * fact(n - k))
    why: The same as $\binom{n}{n-k}$, which has fewer factors to multiply: here $n - k$ = [= n - k].

### Pascal's triangle   @m3.binom.pascal
    can: Build Pascal's triangle and connect it to the coefficient formula.

- The rule of the triangle   @m3.binom.pascal.rule
  can: Build a row of Pascal's triangle from the row above.
  bite: Each entry is the sum of the two entries above it, with 1 at both ends. Row 4 is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Row $n$ lists $\binom{n}{0}, \binom{n}{1}, \ldots, \binom{n}{n}$, so it is also the list of coefficients of $(a + b)^n$.
  ? typed: Row 4 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Write row 5, with commas.
    = 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1
    = 1,5,10,10,5,1
  ? numeric: What do the entries of row [n = 5 | 4 | 6 | 7 | 8] add up to?
    = 2^n
    why: Every row sums to $2^n$: all the ways to pick from $n$ factors.
  ? numeric: In row 6, what is the entry after 1 and 6?
    = 15

- Why the rule holds   @m3.binom.pascal.lcd
  can: Prove the triangle's rule from the explicit form of a coefficient.
  bite: Write a coefficient as $\frac{N!}{A!\,B!}$ with $A + B = N$. The triangle's rule says two neighbors add to the one below: $\frac{N!}{A!\,B!} + \frac{N!}{(A+1)!\,(B-1)!} = \frac{(N+1)!}{(A+1)!\,B!}$. To add the fractions, use the least common denominator $(A+1)!\,B!$. A factorial is built from the one before, $(A+1)! = (A+1) \cdot A!$, so the first fraction needs a factor $(A+1)$ on top and bottom and the second needs a factor $B$. The tops add to $N!\,(A + 1 + B) = N!\,(N+1) = (N+1)!$. For example $\frac{13!}{9!\,4!} + \frac{13!}{10!\,3!} = \frac{14!}{10!\,4!}$: 715 + 286 = 1001.
  ? choice: To add $\frac{N!}{A!\,B!}$ and $\frac{N!}{(A+1)!\,(B-1)!}$, what is the least common denominator?
    (x) $(A+1)!\,B!$
    ( ) $A!\,B!$
    ( ) $A!\,(B-1)!$
    ( ) $(A+1)!\,(B-1)!\,A!\,B!$
  ? choice: What factor does the first fraction, $\frac{N!}{A!\,B!}$, need on top and bottom?
    (x) $(A+1)$
    ( ) $B$
    ( ) $(N+1)$
    ( ) $A$
  ? row: Simplify to a single binomial coefficient and give its value.
    | $\frac{4!}{2!\,2!} + \frac{4!}{3!\,1!}$          = 10
    | $\frac{5!}{3!\,2!} + \frac{5!}{4!\,1!}$          = 15
    | $\frac{8!}{6!\,2!} + \frac{8!}{7!\,1!}$          = 36
    | $\frac{22!}{3!\,19!} + \frac{22!}{2!\,20!}$      = 1771
  ? numeric: $\frac{22!}{3!\,19!} + \frac{22!}{2!\,20!} = \frac{N!}{3!\,20!}$ for which $N$?
    = 23
  ? numeric: Work from the inside out: $\frac{2!}{0!\,2!} + \frac{2!}{1!\,1!} + \frac{3!}{2!\,1!} + \frac{4!}{3!\,1!} + \frac{5!}{4!\,1!} + \frac{6!}{5!\,1!}$. What single value is it?
    = 21
    why: Each addition steps down the triangle: the whole sum is $\frac{7!}{5!\,2!}$. It is also 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6.

- Multiplying a row by (A + B)   @m3.binom.pascal.mult
  can: Explain why multiplying an expansion by $(A + B)$ gives the next row.
  bite: Take the expansion of $(A+B)^3$ and multiply it by $(A + B)$. Distribute: $A$ times the whole row, plus $B$ times the whole row. Multiplying by $A$ raises every power of $A$ by one. Multiplying by $B$ raises every power of $B$ by one. A term such as $A^2B^2$ then arrives twice, once from $A \cdot 3A^1B^2$ and once from $B \cdot 3A^2B^1$, and its coefficient is the sum of those two neighbors: 6. That is the rule of the triangle, seen as like terms being collected.
  ? numeric: In $(A+B) \cdot (A^3 + 3A^2B + 3AB^2 + B^3)$, what is the coefficient of $A^2B^2$?
    = 6
  ? choice: In that product, which two terms of the row combine into the $A^3B$ term?
    (x) $A \cdot 3A^2B$ and $B \cdot A^3$
    ( ) $A \cdot A^3$ and $B \cdot B^3$
    ( ) $A \cdot 3AB^2$ and $B \cdot 3A^2B$
    ( ) $A \cdot A^3$ only
  ? choice: The first expression, $(A+B)$ times the row for the cube, expanded from:
    (x) $(A+B)^4$
    ( ) $(A+B)^3$
    ( ) $(A+B)^2$
    ( ) $A^4 + B^4$

- The binomial theorem   @m3.binom.pascal.thm
  can: Expand a power of a binomial.
  bite: $(a + b)^n$ is the sum of the terms $\binom{n}{k}\,a^{n-k}\,b^k$ for $k$ from 0 to $n$. The powers of $a$ fall from $n$ to 0, the powers of $b$ rise from 0 to $n$, and the coefficients are row $n$ of the triangle. For example $(a + b)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2 b + 3ab^2 + b^3$.
  ? typed: Expand $(a + b)^2$.
    = a^2 + 2ab + b^2
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $a^3 b^2$ in $(a + b)^5$?
    = 10
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $x^2$ in $(x + c)^4$ when $c$ = [c = 3 | 2 | 5]?
    = 6 * c^2
    why: $\binom{4}{2}\,x^2\,c^2$, which is 6 × [= c^2].
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $x$ in $(x + c)^3$ when $c$ = [c = 2 | 3 | 4]?
    = 3 * c^2
    why: $\binom{3}{2}\,x\,c^2$, which is 3 × [= c^2].

- Polynomial coefficients   @m3.binom.pascal.multi
  can: Count and weigh the terms of a power of a sum of several terms.
  bite: In $(A + B + C + D)^5$ each of the five factors contributes one of four letters. A term such as $A^2BCD$ has a polynomial coefficient, the factorial of the total over the factorial of every category: $\frac{5!}{2!\,1!\,1!\,1!}$, a mapping of five factors onto letters with those degrees. A polynomial coefficient is also a product of binomial coefficients, one for each split of a nested two-way tree. Open the one in the figure. The distinct terms are all the ways to share 5 among four exponents, which are the lattice points of a tetrahedron: $\frac{8!}{5!\,3!} = 56$ of them.
  figure: coef 2 1 1 1
  links: @m3.count.slot.map @m3.simplex.lattice.shape
  ? numeric: How many distinct terms does $(A + B + C + D)^5$ have after like terms are collected?
    = 56
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $A^2BCD$ in $(A + B + C + D)^5$?
    = 60
  ? numeric: How many distinct terms does $(A + B + C)^4$ have?
    = 15
    why: Share 4 among three exponents: a triangle of lattice points, $\frac{6!}{4!\,2!}$.
  ? numeric: What is the coefficient of $A^2B^2$ in $(A + B + C)^4$?
    = 6

## Simplex numbers   @m3.simplex

- Running totals   @m3.simplex.diag.sum
  can: Find a running total on one diagonal as a single entry on the next.
  bite: The first diagonal of the triangle is all ones. The second is the natural numbers 1, 2, 3, 4. The third is the triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10: each is a running total of the natural numbers. The fourth is the tetrahedral numbers 1, 4, 10, 20: running totals of the triangular numbers. The fifth is the pentachoral numbers 1, 5, 15, 35: running totals of the tetrahedral numbers. - As binomial coefficients   @m3.simplex.diag.coef
  can: Write a simplex number as a binomial coefficient.
  bite: A diagonal is the set of entries with one fixed factorial underneath. The $n$th triangular number is $\binom{n+1}{2}$, the $n$th tetrahedral number is $\binom{n+2}{3}$, and the $n$th pentachoral number is $\binom{n+3}{4}$. So $1 + 2 + \cdots + 100$ is $\binom{101}{2} = 5050$ with no adding needed.
  ? numeric: What is $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + [n = 20 | 10 | 30 | 40 | 100]$?
    = n * (n + 1) / 2
    why: The triangular number $\binom{n+1}{2}$.
  ? numeric: What is the 5th tetrahedral number, $\binom{7}{3}$?
    = 35
  ? numeric: The sum of the first 6 triangular numbers is which tetrahedral number? Give its value.
    = 56
    why: $\binom{8}{3}$.

### Counting lattice points   @m3.simplex.lattice
    can: Turn a counting problem into a count of lattice points in a simplex.

- Points in a triangle or a tetrahedron   @m3.simplex.lattice.shape
  can: See a simplex number as a count of lattice points.
  bite: Stack dots in a right triangle with 1, 2, 3, … in successive rows and you have a triangular number. Stack those triangles into a pyramid and you have a tetrahedral number. One more dimension gives the pentachoral numbers. A binomial coefficient counts the lattice points in a right simplex, and the lower number of the coefficient is the number of dimensions.
  ? row: In how many dimensions does each shape live?
    slider: 1 | 2 | 3 | 4
    | triangular number      = 2
    | tetrahedral number     = 3
    | pentachoral number     = 4
  ? numeric: A right triangle of dots has 1 dot in its first row, 2 in the next, and so on to [n = 7 | 6 | 9 | 12] in its last. How many dots?
    = n * (n + 1) / 2

- A table split three ways   @m3.simplex.lattice.table
  can: Count the cells of a square table above, on and below its diagonal.
  bite: Take an $n$ by $n$ table with row index $i$ and column index $j$. The cells with $i = j$ are the diagonal: $n$ of them. The cells with $i < j$ make a right triangle of $\binom{n}{2}$ cells, and the cells with $i > j$ make another. Check: $n + 2\binom{n}{2} = n^2$. With three indices, the cells with $i < j < k$ number $\binom{n}{3}$, a tetrahedron.
  ? row: In a 10 by 10 table, how many cells have:
    | $i = j$      = 10
    | $i < j$      = 45
    | $i > j$      = 45
  ? numeric: In an $n$ by $n$ table with $n = [n = 6 | 5..12]$, how many cells have $i < j$?
    = n * (n - 1) / 2
  ? numeric: Three indices each run from 1 to [n = 6 | 5 | 7 | 8]. How many triples have $i < j < k$?
    = n * (n - 1) * (n - 2) / 6
    why: $\binom{n}{3}$.

- Dice   @m3.simplex.lattice.dice
  can: Count the ways to roll a given total without listing them.
  bite: How many ways can 3 dice total 8? Take one pip off each die, so each shows 0 to 5 and the total is 5. Now you are spreading 5 pips over 3 dice: lattice points on a triangle, $\binom{5+2}{2} = 21$. The general count for spreading $s$ pips over $d$ dice is $\binom{s+d-1}{d-1}$. It is exact as long as no die would need more than 5 extra pips. When some could, subtract the arrangements where a die overflows.
  ? numeric: In how many ways can 3 dice total 8? Order matters.
    = 21
  ? numeric: In how many ways can 4 dice total 9?
    = 56
    why: Spread 5 extra pips over 4 dice: $\binom{8}{3}$.
  ? numeric: In how many ways can 2 dice total 7?
    = 6
  ? numeric: In how many ways can 6 dice total 24?
    = 3431
    why: Spread 18 extra pips over 6 dice, $\binom{23}{5} = 33649$, then correct for dice above 5 extra: subtract $6\binom{17}{5}$, add back $15\binom{11}{5}$, subtract $20\binom{5}{5}$. That is 33649 − 37128 + 6930 − 20.

- Tabulating   @m3.simplex.lattice.tab
  can: Lay out every arrangement of a string in a table and see why the table is a simplex.
  bite: To enumerate is to count. To tabulate is to lay every case out so that none can be missed. Tabulate the arrangements of AAAABBB by where the three B's sit: give the table three indices, one for each B's position. - Tabulating three letters   @m3.simplex.lattice.tab3
  scope: extra
  can: Tabulate the arrangements of a string with three different letters.
  bite: For AAABBCC, tabulate in two stages. First place the two B's: any two of the seven places, a triangle of 21 cells. The table is a triangle of triangles with $21 \cdot 10 = 210$ cells, which is the polynomial coefficient $\frac{7!}{3!\,2!\,2!}$. It sits under the first fraction and on top of the second, and cancels. Open the coefficient below the table to see it, and scroll on it to split in another order.
