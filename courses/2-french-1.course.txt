### Core vowels   @fr.sounds.vowels

- Holding a vowel   @fr.sounds.vowels.hold
  can: Produce a steady French vowel and see how close it lands.
  bite: A French vowel is held in one place from start to finish, without the glide an English vowel usually has. Say one and hold it. The chart listens and shows which French vowels your sound is near. Vowels that sound nearly alike share a box, split by a dashed line. A clean vowel lights its cell strongly. An English-colored one lights it faintly and spills elsewhere. Some glow on a neighbor is normal, even for native speakers. Below the chart you can practice a whole word: say it a few times and watch each of its vowels.
  figure: vowels
  ? reveal: Hold a French "a" for two seconds. Did its box light clearly, with little light elsewhere?
    = A steady, open "a" lights the a box almost alone. If "an" lit as well, the sound sat too far back.

- éviter   @fr.words.verbs.eviter
  can: Recall and spell éviter, accent included.
  bite: Something inevitable is something that cannot be avoided. French keeps the same root in plain sight: éviter, to avoid. The accent is part of the spelling, just as much as the letters are.

- Accents are spelling   @fr.words.verbs.accents
  can: Treat a French accent as a letter that cannot be dropped.
  bite: In French, é and e are different letters with different sounds. A word written without its accent is misspelled, in the same way an English word is with a wrong vowel. The keys under an answer box type the accented letters.

    (x) misspelled, because the accent is part of the word

    ( ) correct in capitals only
    ( ) a different verb

### Small words around the verb   @fr.sent.small
    standard: MOD.CLL.NM.1.1 MOD.CLL.NH.1.2
    can: Place object pronouns, contract à with the article, and ask what-questions.

- The pronoun goes before the verb   @fr.sent.small.clitic
  can: Put a direct object pronoun before the verb and make the participle agree with it.
  bite: English says "I saw her", with the pronoun after the verb. French puts it before: Je l'ai vue. Because "la" now stands before the participle, the participle agrees with it and takes an e. Point at each word in the sentence to see why it is there.
  figure: french {see.perf 1s 3f}
  ? typed: Say in French: "I saw her."
    = fr{see.perf 1s 3f}
    why: The pronoun la stands before the verb, loses its vowel before ai, and the participle agrees with it: vue.
  ? choice: In "Je l'ai vue", which earlier word does the participle "vue" agree with?
    (x) l' (that is, la)
    ( ) Je
    ( ) ai
    ( ) none: participles after avoir never agree
    why: A participle after avoir agrees with a direct object that stands before it. Here that is la, feminine, so: vue.
  ? typed: Say in French: "He did not phone me."
    = fr{phone.perf.not 3m 1s}
    why: Ne and pas go around the pronoun and the auxiliary together: ne m'a pas.

  bite: À followed by le is written au, and à followed by les is written aux. Before la or l' nothing changes. Point at "aux" in the sentence to open it back into its two words.

- Asking "what"   @fr.sent.small.que
  can: Ask a what-question in both of its common forms.
  bite: "What is he taking?" can be asked two ways. The everyday form keeps the normal word order after qu'est-ce que. The other form swaps verb and subject and joins them with a hyphen. Use the button under the sentence to see both.
  figure: french {take 3m ?}
  ? typed: Ask in French: "What is he taking?"
    = fr{take 3m ?}
    why: Either form is accepted: Qu'est-ce qu'il prend ? or Que prend-il ?

- A line from a film, taken apart   @fr.sent.small.film
  standard: MOD.CLL.NM.2.1
  can: Find the verbs, pronouns and tenses in a short spoken line.
  bite: In Ratatouille, Rémy scolds his brother: "Émile, tu leur as dit ? C'est ça que j'avais dit de ne pas faire !" Word for word: "Émile, you told them? That is what I had said not to do!" There are four verbs. "As dit" is dire in the passé composé: something done and finished. "Est" is être in the present. "Avais dit" is dire again, one step further back, in the plus-que-parfait: what had already been said before the telling. "Faire" is an infinitive, and its negation "ne pas" stands together in front of it. The pronouns are tu, leur ("to them", before the verb), ce (in c'est), ça, que and je.
  !! The worksheet prompt has "tu leur a dit"; the standard spelling "as" is used here. Context wording is from memory of the film: check it.
  figure: french {say.perf 2s recipient=3p}
  ? match: Match each verb form in the line to its tense.
    | as dit          = passé composé
    | est             = present
    | avais dit       = plus-que-parfait
    | faire           = infinitive
  ? choice: Which of these is NOT a pronoun in the line?
    (x) dit
    ( ) leur
    ( ) ça
    ( ) que
  ? choice: In "tu leur as dit", what does "leur" mean and where does it stand?
    (x) "to them", before the verb
    ( ) "their", before a noun
    ( ) "them", after the verb
    ( ) "to him", before the verb
  ? choice: Why is it "avais dit" and not "ai dit" in the second sentence?
    (x) the saying came before the telling, one step further back in the past
    ( ) the subject is plural
    ( ) it is a question
    ( ) "dire" always takes the imperfect
  ? typed: Where does "ne pas" go with an infinitive? Complete: "J'avais dit de ___ ___ faire."
    = ne pas
  ? typed: Say in French: "You told them."
    = fr{say.perf 2s recipient=3p}

## Shared roots   @fr.roots
   standard: MOD.COD.NL.1.2 MOD.CLL.NH.2.3
   can: Use the Greek and Latin roots that English and French share to recognize and spell words.
   !! From Andrew's conceptual checks. He wants these highlighted: the close lexical relationship between English and French is the lever for the whole course.

### Greek in both languages   @fr.roots.greek
    can: Recognize a word of Greek origin and its Latin counterpart.

- i grec and i latin   @fr.roots.greek.y
  can: Explain why French calls y "i grec".
  bite: The Romans had the letter i. When they borrowed Greek words, they needed a way to write the Greek letter upsilon, a sound Latin did not have, so they borrowed the letter too: Y. French remembers this in the letters' names. Y is "i grec", the Greek i, and i is sometimes called "i latin" to tell them apart. That is why y shows up in words that came from Greek: système, rythme, physique, type, cycle.
  ? choice: Why is the letter y called "i grec" in French?
    (x) it was borrowed into the Latin alphabet to write a Greek letter in Greek words
    ( ) it was invented in Greece for the French language
    ( ) it sounds like the Greek word for "i"
    ( ) Greek has no letter i
  ? choice: A French word is spelled with a y in the middle, like "système". What does that suggest?
    (x) the word probably came from Greek
    ( ) the word is probably a verb
    ( ) the word was borrowed from English
    ( ) nothing; y is as common as i
  ? row: Which is the letter's French name?
    | y      = i grec
    | i      = i latin

- The same Greek word in English and French   @fr.roots.greek.same
  can: Give Greek-origin words that are nearly identical in English and French.
  bite: Words built from Greek came into English and French by the same learned road, so they look almost the same in both. The differences are small and regular: French writes é and è where it says them, ends in -e or -ie where English has nothing or -y, and keeps ph, th, y and ch in the same places. Système and system. Philosophie and philosophy. Théâtre and theater. Rythme and rhythm. Psychologie and psychology.
  ? match: Match each French word to its English twin.
    | système          = system
    | philosophie      = philosophy
    | rythme           = rhythm
    | théâtre          = theater
    | psychologie      = psychology
  ? typed: Write the French for "system", with its accent.
    = système
    ~ systeme :: The letters are right. The second vowel takes a grave accent.
    ~ system :: That is the English spelling. French adds a final e and an accent.
  ? typed: Write the French for "philosophy".
    = philosophie
  ? choice: Which spelling clue most often marks a word as Greek in both languages?
    (x) ph, th, y or ch for a k sound
    ( ) a doubled consonant
    ( ) an ending in -ment
    ( ) a silent final t

- Greek and Latin for the same idea   @fr.roots.greek.pairs
  can: Pair a Greek-origin word or prefix with its Latin counterpart.
  bite: Greek and Latin often built the same idea from their own parts, and English and French kept both. Greek sym-pathie and Latin com-passion are the same construction: "with" plus "feeling". Hypo-thèse and sup-position: "under" plus "placing". Péri-phérie and circon-férence: "around" plus "carrying". Knowing one prefix of each pair gives you two families of words in two languages.
  ? match: Match each Greek prefix to its Latin counterpart.
    | hyper-      = super-
    | hypo-       = sub-
    | poly-       = multi-
    | hémi-       = semi-
    | syn-        = con-
    | péri-       = circon-
  ? match: Match each Greek-origin word to the Latin-origin word built the same way.
    | sympathie        = compassion
    | hypothèse        = supposition
    | périphérie       = circonférence
    | métamorphose     = transformation
  ? choice: "Sympathie" and "compassion" are built from parts meaning:
    (x) "with" and "feeling"
    ( ) "under" and "placing"
    ( ) "around" and "carrying"
    ( ) "many" and "shapes"
