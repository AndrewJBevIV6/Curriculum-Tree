## The variable table   @table
   can: Place the ten dynamic variables in one table and move between neighbors with a single factor.
   !! Source: Unit 1. The table has fifteen neighboring pairs. Each one is a
   !! ladder in @sf, and each topic here links to its ladders.

### The ten variables   @table.vars
    can: Say what each of x, v, a, p, F and θ, ω, α, L, τ measures.

? match: Match each variable to what it measures.
  | velocity             = how quickly position changes
  | acceleration         = how quickly velocity changes
  | momentum             = velocity with the inertia factor counted in
  | force                = how quickly momentum changes
  | angular velocity     = how quickly angle changes
  | torque               = how quickly angular momentum changes

- Position and angle   @table.vars.pos
  can: Say what position x and angle θ measure and in what units.
  bite: Position x says where something is along a path, as a length in meters. Angle θ says how far around a chosen center it is, as a bare number in radians. Neither involves time or an inertia factor, so they are the geometric variables.
  ? choice: Which pair are the geometric variables, involving neither time nor inertia?
    or: Which two variables are of temporal order zero?
    or: Two of the ten variables say only where something is. Which two?
    (x) x and θ
    ( ) v and ω
    ( ) p and L
    ( ) F and τ
    why: Position and angle only say where. Everything else in the table is built from them with time, an inertia factor, or both.

- Velocity and angular velocity   @table.vars.vel
  can: Say what velocity v and angular velocity ω measure and in what units.
  bite: Velocity v is how quickly position changes: meters gained each second, m/s. Angular velocity ω is how quickly angle changes: radians gained each second, written s⁻¹ because the radian is a bare number.
  ? typed: Which variable is the time-rate of angle?
    or: What do we call how quickly an angle changes?
    or: Angle gained each second is which variable?
    = angular velocity
    ~ ω :: Yes. Its name is angular velocity.
  ? choice: In what unit is angular velocity written?
    (x) s⁻¹
    ( ) m/s
    ( ) s⁻²
    ( ) rad
    why: Radians per second, and the radian is a bare number, so only "per second" is left.

- Acceleration and angular acceleration   @table.vars.acc
  can: Say what acceleration a and angular acceleration α measure and in what units.
  bite: Acceleration a is how quickly velocity changes: m/s gained each second, m/s². Angular acceleration α is how quickly angular velocity changes, s⁻². Each is a time-rate of a time-rate.
  ? reveal: Say in one sentence what acceleration measures.
    or: Define acceleration in words, without a formula.
    or: What is an acceleration the time-rate of, and what does that mean in plain words?
    = How quickly velocity changes: the m/s gained each second.
  ? choice: An object keeps a steady [5 m/s]. What is its acceleration?
    (x) 0 m/s²
    ( ) 5 m/s²
    ( ) 5 m/s
    ( ) It cannot be known.
    why: Acceleration is the change in velocity each second, and the velocity is not changing.

- Momentum and angular momentum   @table.vars.mom
  can: Say what momentum p and angular momentum L measure and in what units.
  bite: Momentum p is velocity with the inertia factor counted in: mass times velocity, kg m/s. Angular momentum L is the same for turning: moment of inertia times angular velocity, kg m²/s. Two objects at one velocity share that velocity, and the heavier one has more momentum.
  ? numeric: What is the momentum of a [m = 4 kg | 2..9 kg] object moving at [v = 3 m/s | 2..8 m/s]?
    = m * v
    why: {[m] :: inertia factor} × {[v] :: velocity}.
  ? numeric: What is the angular momentum of a wheel with moment of inertia [I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m²] turning at [w = 2 s⁻¹ | 3..9 s⁻¹]?
    = I * w
    why: {[I] :: inertia factor} × {[w] :: angular velocity}.

- Force and torque   @table.vars.imp
  can: Say what force F and torque τ measure and in what units.
  bite: Force F is acceleration with the inertia factor counted in: mass times acceleration, kg m/s². It is also how quickly momentum changes. Torque τ is the same for turning: moment of inertia times angular acceleration, kg m²/s², and how quickly angular momentum changes.
  ? numeric: What force gives a [m = 5 kg | 2..9 kg] object an acceleration of [a = 3 m/s² | 2..8 m/s²]?
    = m * a
    = m * a -> N
    why: {[m] :: inertia factor} × {[a] :: acceleration}.
  ? choice: {be force timerate(?variable)}
    or: {buildup a.force ?variable over a.duration}
    or: {tell a.force timerate:how(?quantity)}
    (x) momentum
    ( ) velocity
    ( ) acceleration
    ( ) position
    why: Force is to momentum what acceleration is to velocity: the amount gained each second.

### Three relations   @table.rel
    can: Sort the variables by frame, by kind and by temporal order, and find one from its three traits.

? match: Match each word to its meaning.
  | intensive            = shared by every part of a rigid object
  | extensive            = adds up over the parts to give the whole
  | temporal order       = how many times "per second" has been applied
  | path frame           = progress told as a length along the path
  | angular frame        = progress told as an angle about a chosen center

- Path frame or angular frame   @table.rel.frame
  can: Sort the ten variables into path-frame and angular-frame.
  bite: Every variable has a partner in the other frame. Along a path: x, v, a, p, F. Around a center: θ, ω, α, L, τ. The partners play the same role, and a radius stands between them.
  links: @frame.arcangle @sf.radius
  ? row: Name the angular-frame partner of:
    | velocity         = angular velocity
    | force            = torque
    | momentum         = angular momentum
    | position         = angle
    | acceleration     = angular acceleration

- Intensive or extensive   @table.rel.kind
  can: Sort the ten variables into intensive and extensive.
  bite: Intensive variables are shared by every part of a rigid object: x, θ, v, ω, a, α. Extensive variables have the inertia factor counted in, so they depend on how much object there is, and the parts add up to the whole: p, L, F, τ.
  links: @ext.shared @ext.total @sf.inertia
  ? row: Intensive or extensive?
    | angular velocity     = intensive
    | torque               = extensive
    | acceleration         = intensive
    | momentum             = extensive
    | angular momentum     = extensive

- Temporal order   @table.rel.order
  can: Sort the ten variables by temporal order zero, one and two.
  bite: Count how many times "per second" has been applied. Order zero: x, θ. Order one: v, ω, p, L. Order two: a, α, F, τ. Going up an order is taking a time-rate. Going down is letting the quantity build up over a duration.
  links: @frame.rates @sf.time
  ? row: What is the temporal order of:
    slider: -1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3
    | angular momentum     = 1
    | force                = 2
    | angle                = 0
    | angular acceleration = 2
    | velocity             = 1

- Three traits, one variable   @table.rel.locate
  can: Name the one variable that sits where three given traits meet.
  bite: Frame, kind and order together pick out a single cell of the table. Angular, extensive, order two can only be torque. Two cells are empty: there is no extensive variable of order zero in this table, and that gap is where center of mass will go.
  figure: table
  links: @mass.com
  ? row: Which variable is:
    | angular, extensive, order two?     = torque
    | path, intensive, order one?        = velocity
    | path, extensive, order one?        = momentum
    | angular, intensive, order zero?    = angle
    | angular, intensive, order two?     = angular acceleration
    | path, extensive, order two?        = force

### The three factors   @table.factors
    can: Name the factor that separates any two neighbors in the table.
    !! Unit 1 names two factors, time duration and inertia factor. The radius
    !! is added here as the third, for the frame relation.

- Time duration   @table.factors.time
  can: Use a time duration to go between neighboring temporal orders.
  bite: A rate held for a duration builds up a change in the quantity one order below. An acceleration held for some seconds gives a change in velocity. A force held for some seconds gives a change in momentum. The duration is the factor, and it is the same move in every column of the table.
  links: @sf.time
  ? row: Held for a duration, what does each of these build up a change in?
    | velocity             = position
    | acceleration         = velocity
    | force                = momentum
    | torque               = angular momentum
    | angular velocity     = angle

- Inertia factor   @table.factors.inertia
  can: Use a mass or a moment of inertia to go between an intensive variable and its extensive partner.
  bite: Multiplying by the inertia factor crosses from intensive to extensive. Mass does it in the path frame: v to p, a to F. Moment of inertia does it in the angular frame: ω to L, α to τ. Dividing crosses back.
  links: @sf.inertia @table.words.inertia
  ? row: Which inertia factor stands between:
    | acceleration and force?                    = mass
    | angular velocity and angular momentum?     = moment of inertia
    | velocity and momentum?                     = mass
    | angular acceleration and torque?           = moment of inertia

- Radius   @table.factors.radius
  can: Use a radius to go between a path-frame variable and its angular partner.
  bite: For motion around a center, the radius crosses between frames. Position, velocity and acceleration along the arc are their angular partners times the radius. For the extensive pair it runs the other way: angular momentum is momentum along the arc times the radius, and torque is force along the arc times the radius.
  links: @sf.radius @frame.radius
  ? choice: A force of [20 kg m/s²] acts along the rim of a wheel of radius [0.15 m]. How do you get the torque?
    (x) multiply the force by the radius
    ( ) divide the force by the radius
    ( ) multiply by the radius squared
    ( ) the torque equals the force
    why: Torque is the force along the arc times the radius: 3 kg m²/s². The units confirm it.

### Pathways   @table.path
    can: Find a one-step or two-step route across the table and say which givens it needs.

- One step   @table.path.one
  can: Go from one variable to a neighbor with the single factor between them.
  bite: Find the two variables in the table. If they are neighbors, exactly one factor separates them, and the units tell you whether to multiply or divide.
  links: @sf.idea.use
  ? numeric: {accelerate object at [a = 3 m/s² | 2..6 m/s²] for [t = 4 s | 2..5 s]} {change velocity(it) by ?}
    = a * t
    why: {[a] :: acceleration} × {[t] :: duration}. One step down in temporal order.
  ? numeric: A wheel's angular momentum changes by [= tq * t] in [t]. What steady torque did that?
    = tq
    why: {[= tq * t] :: change in angular momentum} ÷ {[t] :: duration}. One step up.
    seed: tq = 51 kg m²/s² | 34 kg m²/s² | 12 kg m²/s² | 85 kg m²/s²
          t = 4 s | 2 s | 5 s | 10 s

- Two steps   @table.path.two
  can: Go between variables two cells apart, and see that both routes agree.
  bite: Variables two cells apart need two factors, and there are two routes. From acceleration to a change in momentum you can cross to force first and then build up over the duration, or build up a change in velocity first and then cross to momentum. Both routes multiply by the same two factors, so they agree.
  ? compound: {accelerate object(mass=[m = 5 kg | 2..9 kg]) at [a = 3 m/s² | 2..6 m/s²] for [t = 4 s | 2..5 s]} {change momentum(it) by ?}
    1. @sf.inertia.path   Cross to force: {[m] :: mass} × {[a] :: acceleration}?      = m * a
    2. @sf.time.imp       Build up over the duration: {force :: step 1} × {[t] :: duration}?      = m * a * t
    = m * a * t
  ? compound: By the other route. {accelerate object(mass=[m = 5 kg | 2..9 kg]) at [a = 3 m/s² | 2..6 m/s²] for [t = 4 s | 2..5 s]} {change momentum(it) by ?}
    1. @sf.time.acc       Build up a velocity change: {[a] :: acceleration} × {[t] :: duration}?      = a * t
    2. @sf.inertia.mom    Cross to momentum: {[m] :: mass} × {velocity change :: step 1}?      = m * a * t
    = m * a * t
  ? numeric: A wheel with moment of inertia [I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m²] has an angular acceleration of [al = 3 s⁻² | 2..6 s⁻²] for [t = 2 s | 2..5 s]. How much does its angular momentum change?
    = I * al * t
    why: Two factors: {[I] :: inertia factor} and {[t] :: duration}, in either order.

- Is the inertia factor needed?   @table.path.need
  can: Decide whether a problem needs the mass or moment of inertia at all.
  bite: If the start and the finish are both intensive, or both extensive, the route never crosses the inertia factor, and a mass given in the problem is not used. Check where the two variables sit before reaching for it.
  ? choice: A [5 kg] object accelerates at [3 m/s²] for [4 s]. You want its <<change in velocity | velocity change>>. Is the mass needed?
    or: Asked only for the <<velocity change | gain in velocity>> of a [5 kg] object that accelerates at [3 m/s²] for [4 s], do you need its mass?
    (x) No. Acceleration and velocity are both intensive.
    ( ) Yes. Every motion problem needs the mass.
    ( ) Yes. Velocity is extensive.
    ( ) Only if the object is turning.
    why: Acceleration to velocity is one step in temporal order. The route stays on the intensive side, so the 5 kg is never used. The change is 12 m/s.
  ? row: Is the inertia factor needed to go from:
    | acceleration to velocity change?            = no
    | acceleration to momentum change?            = yes
    | force to momentum change?                   = no
    | torque to angular acceleration?             = yes
    | angular velocity to angle covered?          = no

### The words   @table.words

? match: Match each of the course's words to what it names.
  | inertia factor       = mass and moment of inertia, taken together
  | impetus              = force and torque, taken together
  | time duration        = the factor between neighboring temporal orders
  | radius               = the factor between a path-frame quantity and its angular partner

- Inertia factor   @table.words.inertia
  can: Use "inertia factor" for mass and moment of inertia together.
  bite: Mass and moment of inertia do the same job in two frames: each says how much impetus it takes to get a given acceleration. "Inertia factor" names them together. Mass is the inertia factor along a path, and moment of inertia is the inertia factor around a center.
  ? reveal: What do mass and moment of inertia have in common, and what is the word for the two together?
    or: Mass and moment of inertia are two cases of one idea. Name the idea and say what the two share.
    = Each is what an intensive variable is multiplied by to give its extensive partner, in its own frame. Together they are the inertia factor.

- Impetus   @table.words.impetus
  can: Use "impetus" for force and torque together.
  bite: Position, velocity, acceleration and momentum each have one name that serves both frames, with "angular" added when needed. Force and torque are the one pair with two unrelated names. In this course "impetus" names them together, to complete the pattern.
  ? typed: What single word does this course use for force and torque together?
    or: Force and torque are the two kinds of what?
    or: Give the frame-neutral name that covers both force and torque.
    = impetus

- "Linear", with care   @table.words.linear
  can: Explain why "path-frame" is safer than "linear".
  bite: The path-frame variables are often called linear, but the path does not have to be a straight line. Motion along a curved arc still has a path-frame velocity in m/s. What separates the frames is the kind of quantity, a length or a bare angle, and the shape of the path does not decide it.
  links: @frame.arcangle
  ? choice: A runner goes round a curved track at [6 m/s]. Is 6 m/s a path-frame or an angular-frame quantity?
    (x) Path-frame. It is a length per second, whatever the shape of the path.
    ( ) Angular-frame, because the track is curved.
    ( ) Neither, because the track is curved.
    ( ) Both at once.
    why: Metres per second measures progress along the path. The angular-frame partner would be in s⁻¹.


## Vector arithmetic   @vec
   can: Add and scale quantities that have directions, with directions on one axis as like terms and directions on different axes as unlike terms.
   !! From this year's early practice. No trigonometry, by design.

### Directions as like and unlike terms   @vec.terms
    can: Tell which directed quantities combine into one term and which must stay apart.

- A direction works like a unit   @vec.terms.dir
  can: Read a direction word as part of a quantity, the way a unit is.
  bite: In [3 m/s² down], the word "down" belongs to the quantity just as m/s² does. It says which way the quantity points along one axis. Up and down share an axis, so "up" is "down" with the opposite sign: [2 m/s² up] and [−2 m/s² down] are one and the same quantity.
  ? choice: Which of these is the same quantity as [2 m/s² up]?
    (x) −2 m/s² down
    ( ) 2 m/s² down
    ( ) −2 m/s² up
    ( ) 2 m/s² right
    why: Reversing the direction word and the sign together changes nothing.
  ? row: Do these two directions lie on the same axis?
    | up and down             = yes
    | up and right            = no
    | left and right          = yes
    | forward and down        = no
    | forward and backward    = yes

- Like terms combine   @vec.terms.like
  can: Add quantities that lie along one axis.
  bite: Quantities along the same axis are like terms, so they combine into one. Opposite directions work against each other: [3 m/s² down] + [1 m/s² up] = [2 m/s² down]. It is the same move as 3x − 1x = 2x, with the direction in the place of x.
  ? numeric: What is [a = 3 m/s² down | 5 m/s² down | 8 m/s² down | 6 m/s² down] + [b = 1 m/s² up | 2 m/s² up | 4 m/s² up]?
    = a + b -> m/s²
    why: Same axis, opposite ways, so the sizes subtract and the larger one keeps its direction: [= a + b -> m/s²].
  ? numeric: What is [p = 4 m right | 7 m right | 10 m right] + [q = 6 m left | 9 m left | 12 m left] + [r = 1 m right | 3 m right]?
    = p + q + r -> m
    why: Collect the terms going right, then take off the one going left: [= p + r -> m] against [q].

- Unlike terms stay apart   @vec.terms.unlike
  can: Keep quantities along different axes as separate terms.
  bite: Up and right lie on different axes, so [3 m right] and [4 m up] are unlike terms. They do not merge into one number, any more than 3x + 4y does. The sum is simply "3 m right and 4 m up", two terms side by side. A third axis, forward and backward, adds a third kind of term.
  ? choice: What is [3 m right] + [4 m up]?
    (x) 3 m right and 4 m up. It stays as two terms.
    ( ) 7 m
    ( ) 7 m up and right
    ( ) 1 m up
    why: Different axes are unlike terms, like 3x + 4y.
  ? row: Like terms or unlike terms?
    | [2 N up] and [5 N down]                   = like
    | [2 N up] and [5 N right]                  = unlike
    | [3 m/s forward] and [3 m/s backward]      = like
    | [4 m left] and [4 m forward]              = unlike

### Adding and scaling   @vec.arith
    can: Add vectors axis by axis and scale a vector by a single factor.

- Adding axis by axis   @vec.arith.add
  can: Add two vectors by collecting like terms on each axis.
  bite: To add vectors, collect the like terms on each axis and leave the axes apart. ([3 m right] and [2 m up]) + ([5 m left] and [4 m up]) gives [2 m left] and [6 m up].
  ? row: A walk goes [a = 3 m right | 6 m right | 8 m right] and [b = 2 m up | 1 m up | 5 m up], then [c = 5 m left | 4 m left | 10 m left] and [d = 4 m up | 3 m up | 7 m down]. What is the total displacement:
    | along the left and right axis?       = a + c -> m
    | along the up and down axis?          = b + d -> m
    why: Left and right: [a] + [c]. Up and down: [b] + [d]. The two answers stay side by side as one displacement.

- The net of opposing forces   @vec.arith.net
  can: Find a net force from forces along one axis, and the acceleration it gives.
  bite: Forces on one object along one axis are like terms. A hanging block with [44 N up] of tension and [49 N down] of weight has a net force of [5 N down]. The relative slack in the tension is what lets it move downward.
  links: @ext.eqacc.g @table.vars.imp
  ? numeric: A hanging block feels a tension of [T = 44 N up | 30 N up | 90 N up] and a weight of [W = 49 N down | 39.2 N down | 98 N down]. What is the net force on it?
    = T + W -> N
    why: [T] + [W] = [= T + W -> N].
  ? numeric: A [m = 5 kg | 4 kg | 10 kg] block hangs from a rope. Gravity pulls it with [= m * g -> N] and the tension in the rope is [T = 44 N up | 30 N up | 90 N up]. What is its acceleration?
    = (T + m * g) / m -> m/s²
    why: Net force [= T + m * g -> N], shared over [m].
    seed: g = 9.8 m/s² down

- Scaling keeps the direction   @vec.arith.scale
  can: Multiply a directed quantity by a factor that has no direction.
  bite: A factor with no direction of its own, such as a duration or a mass, changes the size of a directed quantity and leaves its direction alone. A velocity of [3 m/s down] held for [4 s] gives a displacement of [12 m down].
  links: @sf.time.vd @sf.time.comp
  ? numeric: A velocity of [v = 3 m/s down | 4 m/s up | 6 m/s left] is held for [t = 4 s | 2 s | 5 s]. What displacement does that give?
    = v * t -> m
    why: {[v] :: velocity} × {[t] :: duration}. The direction carries through.

### Components on a coordinate system   @vec.comp
    can: Read a vector as parts along two chosen axes, and say when trigonometry is and is not needed.

- Reading components   @vec.comp.read
  can: Describe how a vector's components depend on the axes chosen.
  bite: Any vector can be told as two parts along two chosen axes. The axes are a choice: turn them, and the same vector reads as different parts, while its size and where it points have not changed. When it lies exactly along one axis, its part along the other is zero.
  figure: components 1.8 kN; axes A, B
  ? choice: A coordinate system is turned while a force stays exactly as it is. What changes?
    (x) the force's components along the axes
    ( ) the force's magnitude

## Reference frames   @frame

### Arc length versus angle   @frame.arcangle
    can: Explain why an arc length and an angle are different kinds of quantity even when they trace the same shape.

- Same shape, different kinds   @frame.arcangle.kinds
  can: Tell an arc length from an angle drawn on the same curve.
  bite: An arc and the angle it spans can be drawn with one stroke of the pen, yet they are different kinds of quantity. The arc is a length: stretch the picture and it grows. The angle is a bare number: stretch the picture and it stays the same.
  links: @sf.idea.equiv
  ? choice: A drawing of a circle with a marked arc is enlarged to twice its size. What happens to the arc length and to the angle?
    (x) The arc length doubles and the angle stays the same.
    ( ) Both double.
    ( ) Both stay the same.
    ( ) The angle doubles and the arc length stays the same.
    why: Arc length is a length and scales with the picture. Angle is arc over radius, and both doubled.

- A chosen center   @frame.arcangle.center
  can: Describe progress along a path either as a distance or as an angle seen from a chosen center.
  bite: Progress along any path can be told as a distance. Choose a center, and the same progress can also be told as the angle swept as seen from there. The center is a choice. Move it, and the same motion sweeps a different angle.
  ? reveal: A car drives 100 m along a road. What extra thing must you choose before you can say what angle it covered?

### Angle as arc over radius   @frame.ratio

  bite: An angle is the arc length measured against the radius: how many radii fit along the arc. A length over a length leaves a bare number, and that is why an angle has no dimension.

### Concentric circles   @frame.concentric
    can: Compare equal path speed with equal angular speed on circles of different size.

- Same angular speed   @frame.concentric.rigid
  can: Find the path speeds of points that turn together at different radii.
  bite: Points on one rigid object, like the hand of a clock, stay lined up and finish a turn together. They share one angular speed. Their path speeds differ in proportion to their radii: a point twice as far out covers twice the arc in the same time.
  links: @sf.radius.vel @ext.angular
  ? numeric: A rigid bar turns about one end. A point [r1 = 0.5 m | 0.2 m | 0.25 m] from that end moves at [v = 3 m/s | 2 m/s | 5 m/s]. How fast does a point [r2 = 2 m | 1 m | 1.5 m] from the end move?
    = v / r1 * r2
    why: Shared angular speed: {[v]} ÷ {[r1]} = [= v / r1]. At [r2] that is [= v / r1 * r2].
  ? choice: Two points on a spinning disk, one near the center and one at the rim. What do they share?

    (x) angular velocity
    ( ) path speed
    ( ) arc length covered each second
    ( ) nothing
    why: They complete each turn together. The rim point covers more arc doing it.

- Same path speed   @frame.concentric.path
  can: Compare the angular speeds of objects moving equally fast on circles of different size.
  bite: Two runners at the same path speed on different circles do not stay lined up. The larger circle has more circumference, so its runner takes longer to get round and has the smaller angular speed. This is why track lanes are staggered: the outer lanes are longer.
  ? numeric: Two runners each move at [v = 6 m/s | 4 m/s | 8 m/s | 12 m/s], one on a circle of radius [r1 = 2 m | 1 m] and one on a circle of radius [r2 = 6 m | 4 m | 8 m]. What is the angular speed of the runner on the larger circle?
    = v / r2
    why: {[v] :: path speed} ÷ {[r2] :: radius}. The inner runner has [= v / r1].

    why: Half a revolution is π radians. The arcs are π × [r1] and π × [r2], so the difference is π × [= r2 - r1 -> m].

### Where the radius enters   @frame.radius
    can: Say where each variable sits by its power of length, and so which way the radius enters between partners.
    links: @table.factors.radius @mass.ring.I

- Powers of length   @frame.radius.count
  can: Place each variable by the power of length in its dimension, and read the frame relation off the difference.
  bite: Count the powers of length in each variable's unit. Angle, angular velocity and angular acceleration have none: they sit at degree 0. Position, velocity and acceleration have one, and so do momentum and force, since mass brings no length with it: degree 1. Angular momentum and torque have two: degree 2. So crossing from the path frame to the angular frame lowers an intensive variable by one degree, from 1 to 0, and raises an extensive variable by one degree, from 1 to 2. That is why the radius divides in the first case and multiplies in the second. The inertia factors account for the difference: mass sits at degree 0 and moment of inertia at degree 2.
  figure: table length
  ? row: At which power of length does each variable sit?
    slider: 0 | 1 | 2
    | angular velocity        = 0
    | velocity                = 1
    | momentum                = 1
    | angular momentum        = 2
    | angular acceleration    = 0
    | torque                  = 2
    | mass                    = 0
    | moment of inertia       = 2
    why: Read it from the unit. s⁻¹ and s⁻² have no meters. m/s, kg m/s and kg m/s² have one. kg m²/s and kg m²/s² have two.
  ? choice: Going from the path frame to the angular frame, what happens to a variable's power of length?
    (x) It drops by one for an intensive variable and rises by one for an extensive one.
    ( ) It drops by one for every variable.
    ( ) It rises by one for every variable.
    ( ) It stays the same.
    why: Velocity (degree 1) goes to angular velocity (degree 0). Momentum (degree 1) goes to angular momentum (degree 2). The two extra degrees on the extensive side are the two factors of radius in the moment of inertia.
  ? choice: On a circle of radius [0.5 m], which is right?
    (x) arc velocity = angular velocity × 0.5 m, and torque = force × 0.5 m
    ( ) arc velocity = angular velocity × 0.5 m, and torque = force ÷ 0.5 m
    ( ) arc velocity = angular velocity ÷ 0.5 m, and torque = force × 0.5 m
    ( ) arc velocity = angular velocity ÷ 0.5 m, and torque = force ÷ 0.5 m
    why: Each product climbs one degree of length: s⁻¹ times m gives m/s, and kg m/s² times m gives kg m²/s².


## Extensivity   @ext
   can: Reason about a rigid object in parts: what the parts share and what adds up.
   !! Source: Unit 3.

### A rigid object in parts   @ext.parts
    can: Describe a partitioned rigid object and why its parts cannot slide against each other.

- One object, many parts   @ext.parts.rigid
  can: Say what it means for a partitioned object to stay rigid, and name the variable that rigidity holds at zero.
  bite: Picture a solid disk divided like a pie chart, the slices painted different colors or cut and glued back. It is still one rigid object. Rigid means no slice moves relative to another. In the language of the variable table: the relative displacement between any two parts, their $\Delta x$ measured one from the other, is zero at all times. The whole object may still turn, and how to describe that turning is a separate choice.
  ? reveal: A disk is cut into slices and glued back together. What must be true of the slices for it to count as one rigid object?
    or: What condition makes a set of glued slices count as a single rigid object?
    = None of them moves relative to any other. Whatever change in position one has, they all have, so the relative displacement between any two is zero.
  ? choice: "No part moves relative to any other." Which variable is that sentence setting to zero?
    or: Rigidity is a statement that one variable, taken between any two parts, is always zero. Which one?
    (x) the relative displacement between the parts, Δx
    ( ) the velocity of each part, v
    ( ) the momentum of each part, p
    ( ) the net force on each part, F
    why: The parts may all move, with a shared velocity, and each has its own momentum and force. What stays zero is the change in position of one part as measured from another.

### Shared quantities   @ext.shared
    can: Explain why velocity and acceleration are shared by every part and by the whole.

- Shared velocity and acceleration   @ext.shared.va
  can: State the velocity or acceleration of a part from that of the whole.
  bite: If no part moves relative to another, every part has the same change in position, so every part has the same velocity and the same acceleration. These are shared by the parts and by the whole. The size of the part does not come into it.
  links: @table.rel.kind
  ? numeric: A [M = 12 kg | 8 kg | 20 kg] disk slides along at [v = 3 m/s | 2..9 m/s]. One slice is a quarter of the disk. What is the velocity of that slice?
    = v
    ~ v / 4 :: Velocity is shared, so it is not divided among the parts. A quarter slice moves exactly as fast as the disk.
    why: Velocity is intensive. Every part has the whole's velocity.
  ? choice: A rigid block accelerates at [2 m/s²]. It is made of a large part and a small part. Which has the greater acceleration?
    or: One rigid block, accelerating at [2 m/s²], consists of a large part and a small part. Which part accelerates more?
    (x) Neither. They share it.
    ( ) the large part
    ( ) the small part
    ( ) It depends on their masses.
    why: Otherwise one would pull away from the other and the block would not be rigid.

- No "total velocity"   @ext.shared.nototal
  can: Say which quantities have a total and which have a shared value.
  bite: We speak of a shared velocity and a shared acceleration, and of a total momentum and a total force. Adding up the velocities of the slices gives a number that depends on how many slices you happened to cut, so it means nothing physical.
  ? row: Shared or total?
    | velocity         = shared
    | momentum         = total
    | acceleration     = shared
    | net force        = total
    | mass             = total

### Total quantities   @ext.total
    can: Add the momentum or force of the parts to get the whole, and find a part's share from its extent.

? match: Match each word to its meaning.
  | shared               = one value that every part and the whole have in common
  | total                = the sum of the values of the parts
  | rigid                = no part moves relative to any other
  | proportional to extent    = a part with a quarter of the mass has a quarter of it

- The parts sum to the whole   @ext.total.add
  can: Add the extensive quantities of the parts to get the whole.
  bite: Each part's momentum is its own mass times the shared velocity. Add them up, and because the velocity is common it comes out of the sum: the total momentum is the total mass times the shared velocity. The same holds for force with the shared acceleration. However the object is partitioned, the parts sum to the same whole.
  links: @mass.inertia.sum
  ? numeric: A rigid object is made of parts of [m1 = 2 kg | 1..4 kg], [m2 = 3 kg | 5..6 kg] and [m3 = 7 kg | 8..10 kg], all moving at [v = 3 m/s | 2..5 m/s]. What is its total momentum?
    = (m1 + m2 + m3) * v
    why: Add the parts, or at once: {[= m1 + m2 + m3] :: total mass} × {[v] :: shared velocity}.

- A part's share   @ext.total.share
  can: Find a part's momentum or force from its share of the whole.
  bite: Since the velocity is shared, a part's momentum is in proportion to its mass. A slice with a quarter of the mass carries a quarter of the momentum and feels a quarter of the net force.
  ? numeric: A [M = 12 kg | 8 kg | 20 kg] disk has a total momentum of [p = 36 kg m/s | 24 kg m/s | 60 kg m/s | 48 kg m/s]. One slice is a quarter of the disk. What is the momentum of that slice?
    = p / 4
    why: A quarter of the mass, the same velocity, so a quarter of the momentum.
  ? numeric: A net force of [F = 24 kg m/s² | 36 kg m/s² | 60 kg m/s²] acts on a [M = 12 kg | 6 kg] rigid disk. What net force acts on a slice of mass [ms = 3 kg | 2 kg | 1 kg]?
    = F / M * ms
    why: Shared acceleration: [= F / M]. The slice needs {[ms]} × {[= F / M]}.

### Equal acceleration   @ext.eqacc
    can: Conclude that two objects with the same acceleration feel forces proportional to their masses, as in surface gravity.

- Forces in proportion to mass   @ext.eqacc.prop
  can: Compare the forces on two objects that accelerate together.
  bite: If two separate objects have the same acceleration, the force on each must be in proportion to its mass. Ten times the mass needs ten times the force to keep up.
  ? numeric: Two objects accelerate side by side at the same rate. One has a mass of [m1 = 2 kg | 3 kg | 4 kg] and feels [= m1 * a]. The other has a mass of [m2 = 6 kg | 8 kg | 10 kg]. What force does it feel?
    = m2 * a
    why: The shared acceleration is [a], so the second needs {[m2]} × {[a]}. Force in proportion to mass.
    seed: a = 5 m/s² | 2 m/s² | 3 m/s²

- Surface gravity   @ext.eqacc.g
  can: Explain why all objects fall with the same acceleration near the ground.
  bite: Near the Earth's surface, gravity pulls on each object in proportion to its mass. That is exactly the condition for equal acceleration, so everything in free fall shares about 9.8 m/s² downward. This shared value is the surface gravity.
  links: @force.calc.g
  ? numeric: What is the gravitational force on a [m = 5 kg | 2..12 kg] object near the Earth's surface, where free fall is [g]?
    = m * g
    why: {[m] :: mass} × {[g] :: surface gravity}, downward. In newtons the number is the same.
    seed: g = 9.8 m/s²
  ? choice: A [1 kg] ball and a [10 kg] ball are dropped together, with no air in the way. Which is right?
    (x) The heavier ball feels ten times the force, and they fall together.
    ( ) They feel the same force, and they fall together.
    ( ) The heavier ball feels ten times the force and falls ten times as fast.
    ( ) They feel the same force, and the lighter one falls faster.
    why: Force in proportion to mass is what equal acceleration requires.

### Equal force   @ext.eqforce
    can: Conclude that two objects under equal forces accelerate in inverse proportion to their masses, as around a barycenter.
    links: @force.third

- Accelerations in inverse proportion   @ext.eqforce.inv
  can: Compare the accelerations of two objects that feel forces of equal size.
  bite: If two objects feel forces of the same size, the one with more mass accelerates less, in inverse proportion. Three times the mass, a third of the acceleration.
  ? numeric: Two carts push off each other, so each feels a force of the same size. The [m1 = 2 kg | 1 kg | 3 kg] cart accelerates at [a1 = 9 m/s² | 6 m/s² | 12 m/s²]. What is the size of the acceleration of the [m2 = 6 kg | 4 kg | 12 kg] cart?
    = m1 * a1 / m2
    why: The force on each is [= m1 * a1]. Shared over [m2], that gives [= m1 * a1 / m2].

- Around a barycenter   @ext.eqforce.bary
  can: Explain why a planet barely moves while its moon swings round.
  bite: A planet and its moon pull on each other with forces of equal size. The moon, with little mass, accelerates a great deal. The planet accelerates too, but only slightly. Both circle a common point, the barycenter, which sits close to the heavier body.
  links: @force.third.acc @mass.com
  ? numeric: The Earth has about [81] times the mass of the Moon, and each pulls the other with equal force. The Moon's acceleration is how many times the Earth's?
    slider: factor powers 81
    = 81
    why: Equal force, so acceleration in inverse proportion to mass.
  ? reveal: The Earth pulls the Moon exactly as hard as the Moon pulls the Earth. Why does it look as if only the Moon is being moved?
    = The forces are equal and the masses are not. The same force gives the Earth about 1/81 of the Moon's acceleration, too little to notice.

### The angular counterparts   @ext.angular
    can: Apply shared and total to angular velocity, angular momentum, angular acceleration and torque.

- Shared and total, turning   @ext.angular.rot
  can: State what the parts of a turning rigid object share and what adds up.
  bite: Everything said about the sliding disk holds for a turning one. The parts share one angular velocity and one angular acceleration. Each part has its own moment of inertia, and so its own angular momentum and its own torque, and those sum to the whole.

## Fundamental forces   @force

### The four forces   @force.four
    can: Rank the four fundamental forces by strength and say which have infinite range.

? match: Match each term to its meaning.

  | fundamental force    = one of the four that every observed force comes down to
  | relative strength    = how strong a force is, with the strong nuclear force set to 1
  | order of magnitude   = one factor of ten

- Four forces   @force.four.names
  can: Name the four fundamental forces.
  bite: Every force ever observed comes down to four: gravity, the electromagnetic force, the strong nuclear force and the weak nuclear force. The two nuclear forces act only inside atomic nuclei, so everything you can see or feel being pushed or pulled is gravity or electromagnetism.
  ? reveal: Name the four fundamental forces.
    or: List the four fundamental forces of nature.

    = Gravity, electromagnetic, strong nuclear, weak nuclear.
  ? choice: Which two fundamental forces account for every force in an everyday mechanics problem?
    (x) gravity and electromagnetism
    ( ) gravity and friction
    ( ) the strong and weak nuclear forces
    ( ) electromagnetism and the strong nuclear force
    why: The nuclear forces do not reach beyond the nucleus. Friction is electromagnetic.

- Relative strength   @force.four.strength
  can: Put the four forces in order of strength.
  bite: Strongest first: strong nuclear, then electromagnetic at about a hundredth of that, then the weak force some eleven orders of magnitude further down, then gravity another twenty-five orders below that. Gravity is by far the weakest. It dominates at large scales only because it always attracts and is never cancelled.
  ? rank: Drag each force to its mark on the band of relative strength, with the strong nuclear force set to 1.
    scale: log
    | strong nuclear      = 1
    | electromagnetic     = 1e-2
    | weak nuclear        = 1e-13
    | gravity             = 1e-38
    why: Electromagnetism is about a hundredth of the strong force. The weak force is eleven orders of magnitude below that, and gravity another twenty-five below the weak force.
  ? choice: Which is the weakest of the four fundamental forces?
    or: Of the four fundamental forces, which has the smallest relative strength?
    or: Order the four fundamental forces by strength. Which comes last?
    (x) gravity
    ( ) the weak nuclear force
    ( ) electromagnetism
    ( ) the strong nuclear force
    why: Gravity is weaker than the weak force by about twenty-five orders of magnitude.

- Range   @force.four.range
  can: Say which forces reach across any distance and which do not.
  bite: Gravity and electromagnetism have infinite range. They weaken with the square of the separation but never reach zero, so every mass pulls on every other mass in the universe. The strong and weak forces have an extremely short, finite range, which is why they never appear in a Newtonian problem.
  ? row: Infinite range or short range?
    | gravity                  = infinite
    | strong nuclear           = short
    | electromagnetic          = infinite
    | weak nuclear             = short

### Electromagnetism in everyday forces   @force.em
    can: Trace contact, friction, tension and muscle forces back to electromagnetic interaction.

- Contact forces   @force.em.contact
  can: Identify everyday pushes and pulls as electromagnetic.
  bite: When you press on a table, the charges in your hand and in the table repel before the atoms can overlap. That is all a contact force is. Friction, tension in a rope, the stiffness of a solid, surface tension, glue, and each step in the working of a muscle are electromagnetic interactions between charges.
  ? row: Which fundamental force is behind:
    | friction between a box and the floor?        = electromagnetic
    | tension in a rope?                           = electromagnetic
    | an apple falling from a tree?                = gravity
    | a muscle contracting?                        = electromagnetic
    | a chair holding you up?                      = electromagnetic

- The one exception   @force.em.gravity
  can: Pick out gravity as the everyday force that is not electromagnetic.
  bite: One everyday force cannot be explained by charges: the pull toward the ground. Weight is gravity. In an ordinary mechanics problem, every other force on the diagram is electromagnetic.
  ? choice: A book rests on a table. Two forces act on it. What are their fundamental origins?
    (x) gravity downward, electromagnetic upward
    ( ) gravity both ways
    ( ) electromagnetic both ways
    ( ) gravity downward, strong nuclear upward
    why: Weight is gravity. The table's push is a contact force, so it is electromagnetic.

### Two laws, one pattern   @force.laws
    can: Set Newton's law of gravitation beside Coulomb's law and name what each proportionality says.

- Universal gravitation   @force.laws.grav
  can: State Newton's law of universal gravitation in words and symbols.
  bite: Any two masses attract. The force is in proportion to each mass, and in inverse proportion to the square of their separation. A constant, G, sets the scale: $F = G\,m_1 m_2 / r^2$.
  ? reveal: State in words what the gravitational force between two objects depends on.
    or: Put Newton's law of universal gravitation into a sentence.
    or: Read $F = G\,m_1 m_2 / r^2$ aloud as a statement about proportions.
    = It is proportional to each of the two masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

- Coulomb's law   @force.laws.coul
  can: State Coulomb's law in words and symbols.
  bite: Any two charges push or pull on each other. The force is in proportion to each charge, and in inverse proportion to the square of their separation. A constant, k, sets the scale: $F = k\,q_1 q_2 / r^2$. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
  ? reveal: State in words what the electric force between two charges depends on.
    or: Put Coulomb's law into a sentence.
    or: Read $F = k\,q_1 q_2 / r^2$ aloud as a statement about proportions.
    = It is proportional to each of the two charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.
  ? choice: Gravity only ever attracts. What about the electric force?
    (x) It attracts unlike charges and repels like charges.
    ( ) It only attracts.
    ( ) It only repels.
    ( ) It attracts like charges and repels unlike charges.

- The shared pattern   @force.laws.pattern
  can: Point out what the two laws have in common and the one place they differ.
  bite: Lay the two laws side by side and they are the same sentence. A constant, times one object's amount, times the other's, over separation squared. Mass plays the part in one that charge plays in the other. They differ only in the constant, and in the fact that charge comes in two signs.
  ? row: In the gravitation law, what plays the part of:
    | the charges q₁ and q₂ in Coulomb's law?     = the masses
    | the constant k?                             = G
  ? reveal: What do the gravitation law and Coulomb's law have in common?
    = Both are a constant times the two objects' amounts (masses or charges), divided by the square of their separation.

### Calculating with the laws   @force.calc
    can: Find a gravitational or electrostatic force, and say how it changes when a mass, a charge or the separation is scaled.
    links: @sf.idea

- Scaling a law   @force.calc.scale
  can: Say how the force changes when one quantity in the law is scaled.
  bite: You rarely need the constant to answer "what happens if". The force follows each mass or charge directly, and follows the separation as an inverse square. Double a mass and the force doubles. Double the separation and the force drops to a quarter.
  ? row: Two masses attract with a certain force. By what factor is the force multiplied if:
    slider: factor 1/9 | 1/4 | 1/3 | 1/2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 9
    | one mass is doubled?                = 2
    | both masses are doubled?            = 4
    | the separation is doubled?          = 0.25
    | the separation is tripled?          = 0.1111
    | the separation is halved?           = 4
    why: Direct with each mass. Inverse square with separation: 1/2², 1/3², and 1/(1/2)² which is 4.
  ? numeric: Two charges repel with [F = 36 N | 18 N | 72 N | 90 N]. One charge is doubled and the separation is tripled. What is the force now?
    = F * 2 / 9 -> N
    why: Times 2 for the charge, times 1/9 for the separation.

- A full calculation   @force.calc.full
  can: Calculate a gravitational or electrostatic force from the law.
  bite: Put the amounts and the separation into the law and carry the units, including the units of the constant. G is 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg². k is 8.99 × 10⁹ N m²/C². The kg² or C² and the m² cancel and newtons are left.
  ? numeric: Two [1000 kg] masses sit [1 m] apart. What gravitational force does each feel? Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg².
    = 6.67e-5 N
    why: G × {[1000 kg]} × {[1000 kg]} ÷ {[1 m]}². A million times G.
  ? numeric: Charges of [2 × 10⁻⁶ C] and [3 × 10⁻⁶ C] sit [0.3 m] apart. What force does each feel? Use k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m²/C².
    = 0.599 N
    why: k × {6 × 10⁻¹² C²} ÷ {0.09 m²}.

- Surface gravity from the law   @force.calc.g
  can: Find the acceleration of free fall from a planet's mass and radius.
  bite: Divide the gravitation law through by the mass of the falling object and that mass is gone. What is left is an acceleration that depends only on the planet: G times the planet's mass over its radius squared. The falling object's own mass was never needed.
  links: @ext.eqacc.g @table.path.need
  ? numeric: The Earth has a mass of [5.97 × 10²⁴ kg] and a radius of [6.37 × 10⁶ m]. What acceleration does its gravity give an object at the surface? Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg².
    = 9.81 m/s²
    why: G × {[5.97 × 10²⁴ kg]} ÷ {[6.37 × 10⁶ m]}². N/kg is the same as m/s².
  ? choice: To find the acceleration of an object falling at the Earth's surface from the law of gravitation, which of these is not needed?
    (x) the mass of the falling object
    ( ) the mass of the Earth
    ( ) the radius of the Earth
    ( ) the constant G
    why: The object's mass is a common factor of the force and of its inertia, so it divides out.

### The third law from symmetry   @force.third
    can: Explain equal and opposite forces from the symmetry of the two expressions, and unequal accelerations from unequal masses.

- A symmetric expression   @force.third.sym
  can: Explain why the two objects in either law feel forces of equal size.
  bite: In both laws the two objects enter in the same way. Swap them, and the expression is unchanged. So the force on the first from the second has the same size as the force on the second from the first, pointing the opposite way. That is Newton's third law, read straight off the formula.
  ? choice: The Earth pulls on a falling apple. How does the apple's pull on the Earth compare?
    or: A falling apple is attracted by the Earth. Compare the force that the apple exerts on the Earth.
    (x) the same size, opposite direction
    ( ) much smaller, because the apple is small
    ( ) zero
    ( ) the same size, same direction
    why: Both masses appear in the law in the same way, so it gives one size for both.
  ? reveal: How does the form of the law of gravitation show Newton's third law?
    = The two masses are multiplied, so swapping the objects changes nothing. Each feels a force of the same size, toward the other.

- Equal forces, unequal accelerations   @force.third.acc
  can: Resolve the puzzle of equal forces that do not look equal.
  bite: It does not look as if the apple moves the Earth. The forces really are equal. What differs is the acceleration, which is the force per unit of mass. The same force shared over the Earth's enormous mass gives an acceleration far too small to see. The intuition that says "unequal" is about acceleration, and it has been filed under the wrong word.
  links: @ext.eqforce.inv @ext.eqforce.bary
  ? numeric: A [m = 0.2 kg | 0.1 kg | 0.5 kg | 0.3 kg] apple falls at [g]. With what force does the apple pull the Earth upward?
    = m * g -> N
    why: The Earth pulls the apple with {[m]} × {[g]}. The apple pulls back equally.
    seed: g = 9.8 m/s²

### Universal constants   @force.const
    can: Say what G, the Coulomb constant and the elementary charge each fix.

- Constants of proportionality   @force.const.Gk
  can: Say what job G and k do in their laws.
  bite: A proportionality says how a force scales. A constant turns it into an actual number of newtons. G does this for gravity and k for electricity. They are universal: the same value everywhere, for every pair of objects. They are scale factors with units, between a combination of amounts and distance on one side and a force on the other.
  links: @sf.idea.dim
  ? choice: Is the constant G dimensionless?
    (x) No. It has units, N m²/kg², so that the law comes out as a force.
    ( ) Yes. It is a pure number.
    ( ) Yes, because it is universal.
    ( ) It depends on the objects.
    why: kg² over m² is not a force. The constant's units make up the difference.

- The elementary charge   @force.const.e
  can: Say what the elementary charge is and how the coulomb relates to it.
  bite: Charge comes in indivisible steps. The smallest is the elementary charge, the size of the charge on one proton or one electron: 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The coulomb is simply a very large number of these, about 6.24 × 10¹⁸.
  ? numeric: How much charge do [5 × 10¹⁸] protons carry? The elementary charge is 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
    = 0.8 C
    why: {[5 × 10¹⁸]} × {1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C}.
  ? choice: Which of these charges is impossible for an isolated object?
    (x) half an elementary charge
    ( ) one elementary charge
    ( ) three elementary charges
    ( ) zero
    why: Charge only comes in whole steps of the elementary charge.


## Scale factors   @sf
   can: Find the factor between two equivalent quantities and use it in either direction.
   !! Source: this year's scale-factor test. Its own numbers are each item's
   !! first value, so the first thing a student meets is something they have
   !! already done.

### What a scale factor is   @sf.idea
    can: Say what a ratio is, find a factor from one pair, and tell whether it has a dimension.

? match: Match each word to its meaning.
  | ratio                = one quantity measured against another
  | scale factor         = the one quantity that carries you between two equivalent quantities
  | dimensionless        = said of a factor whose units cancel completely
  | equivalent           = different in kind, yet each fixes the other in a given setting

- Ratio   @sf.idea.ratio
  can: Say what the word ratio means.
  bite: A ratio is one quantity measured against another: how much of the first there is for each one of the second. Scaling both parts by the same number leaves the ratio alone, so 3 to 7 and 9 to 21 are one ratio written twice.
  ? reveal: What does the word ratio mean?
    or: Explain what a ratio is, in your own words.
    or: What are you saying about two quantities when you state their ratio?
    = One quantity divided by another: how much of the first for each one of the second. It does not change when both are scaled together (3 to 7 is the same ratio as 9 to 21).
  ? choice: Which pair has the same ratio as [3] to [7]?
    (x) 9 to 21
    ( ) 7 to 3
    ( ) 6 to 10
    ( ) 13 to 17
    why: Tripling both parts gives 9 to 21. Adding the same number to both parts (6 to 10, 13 to 17) changes the ratio, and 7 to 3 is the ratio turned over.
  ? numeric: Triangle A has length [L = 3 cm | 2 cm | 5 cm | 4 cm] and height [h = 7 cm | 9 cm | 11 cm]. Triangle B has the same proportions and length [= k * L -> cm]. What is the height of B?
    = k * h -> cm
    why: B's length is [k] times A's, so its height is too: [k] × [h].
    seed: k = 3 | 2 | 5 | 10

- Not equal, but equivalent   @sf.idea.equiv
  can: Explain how two quantities of different kinds can stand for each other in one setting.
  bite: On one particular wheel, [24 cm] of arc and [0.3 rad] of angle describe the same turn. They are not equal, because one is a length and the other is a bare number. They are equivalent: on this wheel, knowing either one tells you the other. What ties them together belongs to the wheel, so it is the same for every other pair on that wheel.
  ? reveal: On one wheel, 24 cm of arc goes with 0.3 rad of angle. Why is it wrong to write 24 cm = 0.3 rad, and what is right to say?
    or: A classmate writes 24 cm = 0.3 rad for a certain wheel. What is wrong with the equals sign, and what should be said instead?
    = They are different kinds of quantity, so they cannot be equal. They are equivalent on this wheel: each one fixes the other through the wheel's radius.
  ? choice: On one wheel, [24 cm] of arc goes with [0.3 rad]. What is the same for every other arc and angle pair on this wheel?
    (x) the arc divided by the angle
    ( ) the arc minus the angle
    ( ) the arc
    ( ) the angle
    why: Arc and angle both change from pair to pair. Their ratio is the radius, and the radius belongs to the wheel.

- Finding the factor   @sf.idea.find
  can: Find a scale factor by dividing one member of a pair by the other, units included.
  bite: One pair is enough. Divide one quantity by the other and carry the units through the division: {[51 kg m²/s²] :: torque} over {[3 s⁻²] :: angular acceleration} is {17 kg m² :: the factor}. Dividing the other way round gives the same factor turned over, and either is a correct answer.
  ? numeric: In one setting, a torque of [tau] goes with an angular acceleration of [alpha]. What is the scale factor between them?
    = tau / alpha
    = alpha / tau
    why: {[tau] :: torque} ÷ {[alpha] :: angular acceleration}. The s⁻² cancels and kg m² is left.
    seed: I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m² | 0.5 kg m²
          alpha = 3 s⁻² | 2..6 s⁻²
          tau = I * alpha
  ? numeric: On one circle, an arc of [= R * th -> cm] goes with an angle of [th]. What is the scale factor between them?
    = R -> cm
    = 1 / R
    why: {[= R * th -> cm] :: arc} ÷ {[th] :: angle, a bare number} leaves a length.
    seed: R = 80 cm | 50 cm | 25 cm | 40 cm
          th = 0.3 rad | 0.5 rad | 0.2 rad | 1.5 rad
  ? numeric: A velocity of [= d / T -> m/s] goes with a displacement of [d]. What is the scale factor between them?
    = 1 / T
    = T
    why: {[= d / T -> m/s] :: velocity} ÷ {[d] :: displacement}. The meters cancel and "per second" is left. Turned over, it is [T].
    seed: T = 0.2 s | 0.5 s | 2 s | 0.1 s
          d = 0.6 m | 1.2 m | 3 m | 0.4 m

- Dimensionless or not   @sf.idea.dim
  can: Decide whether a scale factor is a pure number or a quantity with its own unit.
  bite: Divide the units along with the numbers. If they cancel completely, the factor is a pure number and it compares two quantities of the same kind, such as the two components of one velocity. If a unit is left over, the factor is a quantity in its own right and it carries you from one kind to another: a radius, an inertia factor, a time duration.
  ? row: Is the scale factor between these two dimensionless?
    | [3 m/s] and [−10 m/s]          = yes
    | [24 cm] and [0.3 rad]          = no
    | [51 kg m²/s²] and [3 s⁻²]      = no
    | [0.6 m] and [−2 m]             = yes
    | [3 m/s] and [0.6 m]            = no
    why: Same unit on both sides cancels to a pure number. Otherwise something is left: a length, kg m², a time.
  ? reveal: A scale factor comes out as 17 kg m². It has units. What does that tell you about the two quantities it joins?
    or: Suppose the factor between two quantities is not a pure number but 17 kg m². What follows about the two quantities?
    = They are different kinds of quantity. The factor is itself a physical quantity (here a moment of inertia) and it is what turns one kind into the other.

- Using the factor in both directions   @sf.idea.use
  can: Go either way through a scale factor and check the direction by the units.
  bite: The factor is multiplied in one direction and divided in the other. The units say which. With a factor of 17 kg m², an angular acceleration in s⁻² must be multiplied to become a torque in kg m²/s², and a torque must be divided to come back. If the units of your answer look strange, you went the wrong way.
  ? numeric: The factor between torque and angular acceleration is [I]. What angular acceleration goes with a torque of [= I * a]?
    = a
    ~ I * I * a :: The factor was multiplied in. Look at the units that gives. Torque to angular acceleration divides.
    why: {[= I * a] :: torque} ÷ {[I] :: factor} = [a].
    seed: I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m² | 5 kg m²
          a = 5 s⁻² | 3 s⁻² | 0.6 s⁻² | 20 s⁻²
  ? numeric: The factor between torque and angular acceleration is [I]. What torque goes with an angular acceleration of [a]?
    = I * a
    ~ a / I :: The factor was divided out. Look at the units that gives. Angular acceleration to torque multiplies.
    why: {[a] :: angular acceleration} × {[I] :: factor} = [= I * a].
    seed: I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m² | 5 kg m²
          a = 0.6 s⁻² | 3 s⁻² | 5 s⁻² | 20 s⁻²

- Units on the way through   @sf.idea.units
  can: Re-express a quantity in another unit before or after using a factor.
  bite: A quantity can be re-expressed at any moment without changing it: 0.4 m is 40 cm, one revolution is 2π radians, one minute is 60 seconds. Do it as a step of its own, either before the factor or after, so the factor only ever meets the units it was found in.
  ? row: Re-express:
    | [x = 0.4 m | 0.7 m | 2.5 m | 0.06 m] in centimeters                                        = x -> cm
    | [x = 0.6 mm | 4 mm | 25 mm | 0.3 mm] in centimeters                                        = x -> cm
    | [x = 0.25 revolutions | 0.5 revolutions | 2 revolutions | 0.75 revolutions] in radians     = x -> rad
    | [x = 1.5 RPM | 30 RPM | 0.5 RPM | 12 RPM] in radians per second                            = x -> s⁻¹
    | [x = 0.45 RPM per second | 3 RPM per second | 60 RPM per second] in s⁻²                    = x -> s⁻²
    why: Centimeters: 100 to the meter, 10 mm to the centimeter. Revolutions: times 2π. Per minute to per second: divide by 60.

## Extended masses   @mass
   can: Treat a rigid object as a collection of rings and find how it responds to a torque.
   !! Source: Unit 7.

### Center of mass   @mass.com
    can: Find a center of mass as a mass-weighted average of positions, and tell a weighted sum from a weighted average.

- Weighted sum and weighted average   @mass.com.avg
  can: Tell a weighted sum from a weighted average.
  bite: Multiply each position by the mass sitting there and add: that is a weighted sum, in kg m. Divide it by the total mass and you have a weighted average, back in meters. The masses are the "weights" here in the statistical sense of the word, which is separate from weight as the pull of gravity.
  ? numeric: A [m1 = 2 kg | 1..4 kg] mass sits at [x1 = 0 m | 1 m | 2 m] and a [m2 = 6 kg | 4 kg | 8 kg] mass at [x2 = 4 m | 6 m | 10 m]. What is the mass-weighted sum of their positions?
    = m1 * x1 + m2 * x2
    why: {[m1]} × {[x1]} + {[m2]} × {[x2]}.
  ? choice: What is the difference between a weighted sum and a weighted average?
    (x) The average is the sum divided by the total of the weights.
    ( ) They are the same thing.
    ( ) The sum is the average divided by the total of the weights.
    ( ) The average ignores the weights.

- Finding a center of mass   @mass.com.find
  can: Find the center of mass of point masses on a line.
  bite: The center of mass is the mass-weighted average position. It sits nearer the heavier masses. It is the missing extensive cell of order zero in the variable table, once the total mass is divided back out.
  links: @table.rel.locate @ext.eqforce.bary
  ? numeric: A [m1 = 2 kg | 1 kg | 3 kg] mass sits at [x1 = 0 m] and a [m2 = 6 kg | 3 kg | 9 kg] mass at [x2 = 4 m | 8 m | 12 m]. Where is their center of mass?
    = (m1 * x1 + m2 * x2) / (m1 + m2)
    why: {[= m1 * x1 + m2 * x2] :: weighted sum} ÷ {[= m1 + m2] :: total mass}. It sits nearer the heavier mass.
  ? numeric: Masses of [1 kg], [2 kg] and [5 kg] sit at [0 m], [3 m] and [10 m]. Where is their center of mass?
    = 7 m
    why: Weighted sum 0 + 6 + 50 = 56 kg m, over 8 kg.
    vary: numbers

### Center of mass and momentum   @mass.ensemble
    can: Relate the motion of the center of mass to the total momentum of the collection.

- The velocity of the whole   @mass.ensemble.p
  can: Find the velocity of a collection's center of mass from its total momentum.
  bite: Total momentum is the mass-weighted sum of the velocities, just as the center of mass comes from the mass-weighted sum of the positions. They are the same construction one temporal order apart. So the total momentum over the total mass is the velocity of the center of mass: the one velocity that speaks for the whole collection.
  links: @ext.total.add @table.rel.order
  ? numeric: A [m1 = 2 kg] mass moves at [v1 = 3 m/s | 5 m/s] and a [m2 = 6 kg] mass at [v2 = 1 m/s | 3 m/s] in the same direction. How fast does their center of mass move?

    why: Total momentum [= m1 * v1 + m2 * v2], over [= m1 + m2].

### Moment of inertia as a sum   @mass.inertia
    can: Build a rigid object's moment of inertia by adding mr² over its parts.

- A point mass about an axis   @mass.inertia.point
  can: Find the moment of inertia of a point mass at a given distance from an axis.
  bite: A point mass has an inertia factor for turning about an axis: its mass times the square of its distance from the axis. The same mass counts for more the further out it sits, and the distance counts twice.
  links: @frame.radius.count
  ? numeric: What is the moment of inertia of a [m = 3 kg | 2..6 kg] point mass [r = 2 m | 3 m | 0.5 m | 10 m] from the axis?
    = m * r^2
    why: {[m] :: mass} × {[r] :: distance}².
  ? choice: A point mass is moved to twice its distance from the axis. Its moment of inertia becomes:
    or: Doubling a point mass's distance from the axis makes its moment of inertia:
    (x) four times as large
    ( ) twice as large
    ( ) half as large
    ( ) unchanged
    why: The distance enters squared.

- Adding the parts   @mass.inertia.sum
  can: Add the moments of inertia of parts joined into one rigid object.
  bite: Moment of inertia is extensive. When parts are joined rigidly about one axis, their moments of inertia add, just as their masses do. A pulley made of three rings has the three ring values summed.
  links: @ext.total.add @ext.angular.rot
  ? numeric: Three rings about the same axis have moments of inertia [a = 6750 g cm² | ~1000..9000 g cm²], [b = 490 g cm² | ~100..900 g cm²] and [c = 200 g cm² | ~100..900 g cm²]. They are joined into one rigid pulley. What is its moment of inertia?
    = a + b + c -> g cm²
    why: Extensive quantities of the parts sum to the whole: [a] + [b] + [c].
  ? choice: Two rings are joined into one rigid wheel. Which of these do you add to get the wheel's value?
    (x) their moments of inertia
    ( ) their angular accelerations
    ( ) their angular velocities
    ( ) their radii
    why: Moment of inertia is extensive, so it adds. Angular velocity and angular acceleration are shared by every part of a rigid object.

### Rings as building blocks   @mass.ring
    can: Find a ring's moment of inertia from its mass and radius, and its radius from the other two.

- A ring's moment of inertia   @mass.ring.I
  can: Find the moment of inertia of a ring from its mass and radius.
  bite: All of a ring's mass sits at one distance from the axis, so its moment of inertia is simply its mass times that radius squared: $I = m r^2$. The radius counts twice, so a ring ten times wider has a hundred times the moment of inertia. Keep the units exactly as they multiply: grams with square centimeters is fine.
  ? row: How much moment of inertia does a [m = 50 g | 20 g | 4 g | 30 g] ring of mass have:
    | at a radial size of [r = 5 cm | 3 cm | 7 cm | 10 cm]?     = m * r^2 -> g cm²
    | at a radial size of [r = 50 cm | 30 cm | 20 cm]?          = m * r^2 -> g cm²
    | at a radial size of [r = 3 m | 2 m | 5 m]?                = m * r^2 -> g m²
    why: {[m] :: mass} × {radius squared}. The radius counts twice.

- What size ring   @mass.ring.size
  can: Find the radius of a ring from its moment of inertia and its mass.
  bite: Dividing a ring's moment of inertia by its mass leaves the square radius, in m². That already tells you the size. Its square root is the radius itself, in meters.
  links: @mass.gyr.msr
  ? row: What size ring has [I = 500 g m² | 200 g m² | 900 g m²] moment of inertia:
    | with mass [m = 1 kg | 2 kg | 4 kg]?        = sqrt(I / m) -> m
                                                 ~ I / m :: That is the square radius, and it does carry the size. Its root is the radius.
    | with mass [m = 3 kg | 5 kg | 0.5 kg]?      = sqrt(I / m) -> m
                                                 ~ I / m :: That is the square radius, and it does carry the size. Its root is the radius.
    | with mass [m = 5 g | 2 g | 8 g]?           = sqrt(I / m) -> m
                                                 ~ I / m :: That is the square radius, and it does carry the size. Its root is the radius.
    why: Write the mass in grams. {[I] :: moment of inertia} ÷ {mass} gives the square radius in m². Then take the root.

### Mean square radius and radius of gyration   @mass.gyr
    can: Find an object's mean square radius and radius of gyration, and name the single ring that would turn the same way.

? match: Match each term to its definition.
  | center of mass       = the mass-weighted average position
  | moment of inertia    = the mass-weighted sum of square radius
  | mean square radius   = moment of inertia divided by total mass
  | radius of gyration   = the square root of the mean square radius

- Mean square radius   @mass.gyr.msr
  can: Find an object's mean square radius from its moment of inertia and mass.
  bite: Moment of inertia is a mass-weighted sum of square radius. Divide by the total mass and it becomes a mass-weighted average: the mean square radius. It is to moment of inertia what center of mass is to the weighted sum of positions.
  links: @mass.com.avg
  ? numeric: A pulley has a moment of inertia of [I = 7440 g cm² | 5000 g cm² | 9600 g cm²] and a mass of [M = 42 g | 40 g | 50 g | 30 g]. What is its mean square radius?
    = I / M -> cm²
    why: {[I] :: moment of inertia} ÷ {[M] :: mass}.

- Radius of gyration   @mass.gyr.k
  can: Find an object's radius of gyration.
  bite: The square root of the mean square radius is a length: the radius of gyration. It is a kind of average distance of the mass from the axis, the root mean square.
  ? numeric: A pulley has a mean square radius of [q = 177.14 cm² | 144 cm² | 225 cm² | 90 cm²]. What is its radius of gyration?
    = sqrt(q) -> cm
    why: The square root of [q].
  ? numeric: A wheel has a moment of inertia of [I = 18 kg m² | 32 kg m² | 50 kg m²] and a mass of [M = 2 kg | 8 kg]. What is its radius of gyration?
    = sqrt(I / M)
    ~ I / M :: That is the mean square radius. Its root is the radius of gyration.
    why: {[I]} ÷ {[M]} = [= I / M], and the root of that.

- The equivalent ring   @mass.gyr.ring
  can: Replace any rigid object by a single ring that turns the same way.
  bite: For turning about an axis, all that matters about an object is its mass and its radius of gyration. So any object can be swapped for one ring of the same mass at that radius, and nothing about its rotation changes. This is why rings are the building blocks.
  ? choice: A pulley of mass [42 g] has a radius of gyration of [13.31 cm]. Which single ring responds to a torque exactly as the pulley does?
    (x) a 42 g ring of radius 13.31 cm
    ( ) a 42 g ring at the pulley's outer radius
    ( ) a 13.31 g ring of radius 42 cm
    ( ) no single ring can
    why: Same mass, same mean square radius, so the same moment of inertia.

### Shape factors   @mass.shapes
    can: Explain the constant in front of mr² for a disk, sphere or rod from where its mass sits.
    !! Unit 7 names disks, spheres and rods. Trim to what was reached.

- Why a disk is less than a ring   @mass.shapes.disk
  can: Explain why a solid disk has a smaller moment of inertia than a ring of the same mass and radius.
  bite: A solid disk is like a ring whose mass has been spread inward, all the way to the axis. On average its mass sits closer in, so its mean square radius is smaller than the outer radius squared. For a uniform disk it is exactly half: $I = \tfrac12 M R^2$.
  ? numeric: What is the moment of inertia of a uniform solid disk of mass [M = 2 kg | 4 kg | 6 kg] and radius [R = 0.3 m | 0.5 m | 0.2 m]?
    = M * R^2 / 2
    ~ M * R^2 :: That is the ring's value. A solid disk has half of it.
    why: Half of {[M]} × {[R]}².
  ? numeric: What is the radius of gyration of a uniform solid disk of radius [R = 0.3 m | 0.5 m | 0.2 m | 1 m]?
    = R / sqrt(2)
    why: The mean square radius is half of [= R^2], and its root is about 71 % of the outer radius.

- Reading a shape factor   @mass.shapes.table
  can: Read the number in front of MR² as a statement about where the mass sits.
  bite: The number in front is the mean square radius as a fraction of the outer radius squared. Ring: 1, all the mass at the rim. Solid disk: 1/2. Solid sphere: 2/5, with still more of its mass near the axis. A thin rod turning about its center: 1/12 of its length squared.
  ? row: A ring, a solid disk and a solid sphere have the same mass and the same outer radius. Which has:
    | the largest moment of inertia?       = ring
    | the smallest moment of inertia?      = sphere
  ? choice: A ring and a solid disk of equal mass and radius are given the same torque. Which gains angular velocity faster?
    (x) the disk
    ( ) the ring
    ( ) they tie
    ( ) it depends on the torque
    why: The disk has half the moment of inertia, so the same torque gives it twice the angular acceleration.

### Torque over moment of inertia   @mass.alpha
    can: Find a rigid object's angular acceleration from the forces on it and its moment of inertia.
    links: @sf.inertia.rot @sf.radius.imp

- Net torque   @mass.alpha.net
  can: Combine several torques on one object into a net torque with a sense.
  bite: Torque is extensive, so torques on one object combine. Those turning it the same way add. Those turning it opposite ways subtract, and the larger one decides the sense, clockwise or counterclockwise. A force pulling down on the right of the axis turns it clockwise. The same force on the left turns it counterclockwise.
  links: @ext.angular.rot
  ? numeric: One rope gives a pulley [t1 = 30 000 g cm²/s² clockwise | 50 000 g cm²/s² clockwise | 24 000 g cm²/s² clockwise] and another gives [t2 = 20 000 g cm²/s² counterclockwise | 10 000 g cm²/s² counterclockwise | 15 000 g cm²/s² counterclockwise]. What is the net torque, with its direction?
    = t1 + t2 -> g cm²/s²
    why: [t1] + [t2] = [= t1 + t2 -> g cm²/s²]. Opposite senses, so the sizes subtract, and the larger one decides the direction.
  ? choice: Two ropes hang off opposite sides of a pulley and both pull downward. Their torques:
    (x) oppose each other
    ( ) add
    ( ) are both clockwise
    ( ) are both zero
    why: Down on the right turns it clockwise. Down on the left turns it counterclockwise.

- Angular acceleration from torque   @mass.alpha.find
  can: Find the angular acceleration of a rigid object from the net torque and its moment of inertia.
  bite: Angular acceleration is the net torque shared out over the moment of inertia. Torque over moment of inertia: the kg m² cancels and s⁻² is left. More moment of inertia, less angular acceleration from the same torque.
  links: @sf.inertia.rot
  ? numeric: A net torque of [= I * al] acts on a wheel with a moment of inertia of [I = 17 kg m² | 4 kg m² | 12 kg m²]. What is its angular acceleration?
    = al
    why: {[= I * al] :: net torque} ÷ {[I] :: moment of inertia}.
    seed: al = 3 s⁻² | 2 s⁻² | 5 s⁻² | 0.5 s⁻²
